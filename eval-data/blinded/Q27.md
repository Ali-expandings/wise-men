A company published a benchmark showing their AI model outperforms GPT-4 on 14 tasks. They chose the benchmark. They funded the study. How contaminated is this evidence and what would make it credible?

"""
## Answer

The evidence is severely contaminated and should be treated as marketing material rather than scientific proof of superiority. A calibrated reader may take it as weak directional signal — the model might have genuine strengths in some domains — but it provides no defensible basis for a claim of general outperformance.

The contamination operates through several specific, compounding mechanisms. First, and most damaging, is task-selection bias with an unknown denominator: "14 tasks" is an arbitrary slice of a candidate universe the company alone examined. Without knowing how many tasks were considered and rejected, the result cannot be interpreted — this is the classic problem of researcher degrees of freedom applied to benchmark design, and it makes the reported win rate essentially uninterpretable. Second, funding bias produces motivated reporting at every stage: task inclusion, metric choice, prompt formatting, and disclosure of results. Third, training-data contamination is a live risk whenever a benchmark's task distribution was knowable during the training pipeline — strong performance may reflect memorization or near-memorization rather than genuine generalization. Fourth, metric and prompt asymmetry is particularly insidious: the company almost certainly fine-tuned prompts and inference settings for their own model over many iterations, while using off-the-shelf or suboptimal settings for GPT-4, creating an artificial performance gap that has nothing to do with underlying capability. Fifth, there are no confidence intervals, no per-task failure distributions, and no disclosure of tasks where the model performed poorly — all of which are necessary for any honest effect-size claim.

The highest-leverage remediation, and the one that addresses the deepest structural flaw, is a third-party pre-registered evaluation on a held-out benchmark the company did not select or see before the evaluation was locked. The protocol — task set, evaluation metrics, prompt templates, model versions, inference settings, and sampling parameters for both models — must be published on a registry such as OSF before any model inference is run. This single step converts a retrospective narrative, which can always be constructed post hoc to look favorable, into a falsifiable prospective test.

Beyond that foundational requirement, credibility requires the following in priority order. The company must release the complete evaluation harness, every piloted or piloted-and-dropped task (not just the 14 that were published), and the exact prompts used for both models, so that the denominator problem becomes auditable. A contamination audit — n-gram or embedding overlap analysis between the model's training corpus and each test item — must be conducted by the independent evaluator to rule out memorization. Results must include confidence intervals and full per-task score distributions across multiple runs, not point estimates. Neutral funding, meaning the third-party evaluator has no equity stake or financial relationship with the company, is a prerequisite rather than a nice-to-have. Ideally, the evaluation should also include several externally recognized suites — MMLU, BIG-Bench Hard, HellaSwag, or equivalents relevant to the claimed application domain — selected before any scores are observed, to establish that gains on the company's chosen tasks correspond to gains on independently chosen constructs.

Without these elements, the 14-task result is a marketing artifact. With them, it becomes a falsifiable empirical claim.

Confidence in this assessment is high. The conditions present here — self-selected tasks, self-funding, no pre-registration, no independent replication, no contamination audit, and no failure disclosure — are precisely the documented conditions under which AI benchmark results reliably fail to replicate and reliably overstate performance differentials. The failure modes are not theoretical; they are the standard observed pattern in the literature on benchmark gaming in machine learning.

## Dissent worth keeping

The council's near-dismissal overstates the contamination and undervalues what the company actually brings to the table. A company knows its model's failure modes, its intended deployment contexts, and the edges where the model operates at the boundary of its competence — far better than any neutral third-party evaluator who is designing tasks from the outside. A self-chosen benchmark, precisely because it is designed by someone with deep operational knowledge of the model, may probe ecologically valid and practically relevant capabilities that a naive external benchmark would miss entirely. The contamination is real; no one disputes that. But the correct Bayesian update is not to zero. Discount the claimed magnitude — heavily, if you like — but do not discard the directional claim. The finding that this model outperforms GPT-4 on these specific tasks remains informative evidence worth investigating, not evidence worth ignoring. The right response is not to replace the entire question with a different benchmark of someone else's choosing. It is to require the company to release the full evaluation harness, every raw per-task score including the tasks that were piloted and dropped, and the exact prompts used for both models — then have one neutral academic replicator run the identical harness, unmodified, and report whether the original results hold under controlled replication. That tests the integrity of the existing evidence rather than substituting a different experiment in its place.

## Answer

This evidence is heavily contaminated, and the contamination runs through several compounding layers that make the headline claim close to meaningless without further scrutiny.

The most corrosive problem is benchmark selection bias. When a company chooses which tasks to evaluate, they are almost certainly — whether consciously or not — selecting tasks where their model looks strongest. This is not necessarily dishonest; it can happen through a completely natural process of internal testing and then deciding to publish results on the dimensions that looked promising. But the outcome is the same: you are measuring the distance from first base to second base and reporting it as proof the field is smaller than you thought. Fourteen tasks sounds like a lot, but it is an extraordinarily narrow slice of what general AI capability means, and the fact that the company drew the circle around those specific fourteen tells you almost nothing about what would happen if an independent party drew a different circle. If they tested on, say, forty tasks and published only the fourteen where they won, the reported results are technically accurate and profoundly misleading simultaneously.

The funding bias layer sits on top of that. Research consistently shows that industry-funded studies across many fields — pharmaceuticals, nutrition, economics — produce results favorable to the funder at rates far exceeding chance. This is not primarily because researchers fabricate data. It happens through subtler mechanisms: framing choices, preprocessing decisions, which failure modes to investigate, how to handle edge cases, what counts as a tie, and how to write up ambiguous results. When the people running the study are also the people who built the model being tested, the opportunities for these micro-decisions to tilt toward favorable outcomes are essentially unlimited, even with good intentions.

The third layer is that GPT-4 is a moving target and benchmarks can be gamed through training data contamination. If the company's model was trained on data that included the benchmark tasks or closely related problems, performance improves for reasons that have nothing to do with general capability. This is a known and serious problem across the field, not an accusation specific to this hypothetical company.

To make this evidence credible, several things would need to change. First, the benchmark selection would need to be pre-registered by a neutral third party before either model was trained or fine-tuned, so the evaluation criteria could not be chosen in hindsight. Second, the evaluation itself would need to be conducted by researchers with no financial stake in either model, with full access to both systems, running identical protocols. Third, the full results across all tasks evaluated — not just the fourteen favorable ones — would need to be disclosed, with a clear explanation of how the task set was constructed. Fourth, the methodology would need to include adversarial evaluation: deliberately probing for failure modes rather than just measuring peak performance on well-defined tasks. Fifth, the paper would need to go through peer review at a credible venue where reviewers could push back on the task selection rationale.

Even with all of that, a single benchmark study is weak evidence about something as complex as general model capability. What would actually be credible over time is a pattern of results across independent evaluations using diverse methodologies, with different evaluators, under different conditions, some of which the company did not choose and some of which they lost. The absence of that broader picture is itself informative. Companies whose models are genuinely superior tend to welcome third-party evaluation rather than curating their own showcase studies.

## Answer

The evidence is severely contaminated — enough that no confident comparative claim about general model quality can be drawn from it. Contamination operates on two axes simultaneously.

**Task selection bias** is the primary problem. "They chose the benchmark" hides a crucial unknown: the denominator. If the company tested 14 tasks and published 14, that is a different situation from testing 100 tasks and publishing the 14 where they won. Without the full experimental record, you cannot distinguish genuine capability from selection effects. This is survivorship bias baked in by design — the moment you allow a party to choose its own evaluation criteria, you are measuring their ability to find favorable criteria at least as much as you are measuring model quality.

**Funding bias** compounds this. The literature on industry-funded research in pharma, nutrition, and increasingly AI is consistent: funded studies produce systematically more favorable results, not necessarily through fraud, but through thousands of small decisions — what to control for, how to define success, what to report — that accumulate in the funder's favor. The bias is often not conscious.

A third contamination layer is **benchmark overfitting**: if the model was tuned or evaluated internally against similar tasks during development, the benchmark is not measuring held-out generalization, it is measuring in-distribution performance — which tells you almost nothing about real-world deployment.

What would make it credible, in order of importance:

1. **Pre-registration** of the full benchmark specification before model training or fine-tuning concludes — so the model cannot be tuned to the benchmark.
2. **Full disclosure of all tasks tested**, not just the wins. If they tested 14 tasks and all 14 are in the paper, that is stated explicitly. If they tested more, all results must be published.
3. **Third-party independent replication** with full API access or model weights, run by researchers with no financial stake.
4. **Neutral multi-model benchmarks** (HELM, BIG-Bench Hard, MMLU variants administered by independent parties) run in parallel so the results can be contextualized against a shared reference.
5. **Dataset contamination audits** confirming the test set does not overlap with the model's training data.
6. Confidence intervals on each of the 14 tasks, not just win/loss framing.

The absence of any of these does not prove fraud. It proves the evidence cannot carry the weight being placed on it.

## Dissent worth keeping

*"The independence requirement here might actually be counterproductive. A company that knows their model inside out is better positioned to design tasks that reveal real capability differences than a neutral external party who designs tasks that systematically miss what the model actually does well. Cherry-picking is only harmful if the cherry-picked tasks don't correspond to real-world use — but if those 14 tasks happen to be the tasks that genuinely matter in practice, outperforming on them is exactly the signal you want. The adversarial framing of self-evaluation assumes bad faith; domain-expert self-evaluation can be more accurate than naive third-party testing. We should be asking whether the tasks are ecologically valid, not just who designed them."*

## Confidence

**Medium.** The five perspectives converged strongly on "high contamination," but diverged meaningfully on whether any useful signal survives and on whether the remedy is primarily institutional (independent evaluation bodies) or task-level (ecological validity of the chosen tasks) — which leaves the prescription less settled than the diagnosis.
