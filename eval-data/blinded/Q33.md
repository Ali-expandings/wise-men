QUESTION:
"""
My team writes internal docs that nobody reads. They're well-organized and accurate but people still don't consult them before asking questions. Is this a writing problem or a culture problem, and how do I tell?
"""

====================================================================
Response X:
"""
## Answer

This is almost certainly a culture problem wearing a writing problem's clothes, but the distinction matters less than most people think — and the way to tell is by running a small experiment rather than by analyzing the docs themselves.

Here's the core diagnostic: take your three most frequently asked questions from the past month, find the doc that answers each one, and measure how many clicks it takes a new person to get from "I have this question" to "I found the answer." If the answer is more than two clicks, or if the person would need to already know what the doc is called in order to find it, you have a discoverability problem, which is a writing/structure problem. If the answer is one click and the doc is clearly written but people still ask the question, that's a culture problem.

The reason most people misdiagnose this is that they evaluate docs by how good they look when you already know where they are. That's not the user experience. The actual user experience starts with a vague feeling of uncertainty, a quick mental check of "where would I even look for this," and then a decision about whether asking a person is faster than searching. If asking a person has ever worked and taken under thirty seconds, that pathway is reinforced, and no amount of documentation quality changes the calculus.

The honest signs that it's primarily a culture problem: people who wrote the docs also don't consult them and go straight to asking; senior people on your team visibly model the behavior of answering questions from memory rather than linking to docs; there's mild social reward for being the person who knows things, and no social friction for asking questions that are already documented. These are behavioral norms, and they don't respond to better writing.

The honest signs it's primarily a writing/structure problem: your docs are organized around how your team thinks about the system rather than how someone with a question thinks about it; docs are titled with internal jargon that doesn't match how people phrase questions; there's no single obvious starting point, so people face a whole wiki with no clear entry path; and search returns too many results or the wrong ones.

In practice, most teams have both, in roughly this proportion: 30% writing and 40% culture and 30% discoverability. The discoverability piece often gets ignored because it falls between the two — it's not exactly a writing quality issue and it's not exactly a norms issue, but it's actually the cheapest to fix. A well-placed link in a Slack channel description, a pinned message, a simple search alias, or a bot that auto-responds to common questions with doc links can move the needle on discoverability in a day.

The culture piece takes longer and requires modeling from whoever has the most social authority on the team. That person needs to start responding to questions with "great question, the answer is in [link]" rather than just answering, and they need to do it without being condescending about it, which is genuinely hard. Over a few months, people learn that consulting docs is the faster path.

So: look at discoverability first because it's the easiest to fix and will give you a quick signal. If fixing discoverability doesn't move behavior after three or four weeks, you're looking at culture, and you need to change what gets modeled and socially rewarded rather than what gets written.
"""

"""
## Answer

It is almost certainly both, but the interventions are completely different, so you need to distinguish them rather than treat them as equivalent.

The fastest diagnostic is to intercept live incidents rather than theorize. The next time someone asks a question that has a documented answer, stop and ask them directly: "Did you check the docs first?" Their answer sorts the problem immediately. If they say yes and couldn't find it — or found something outdated — that is a writing, structure, or discoverability problem. If they say no, or "I didn't think to," or "it's faster to just ask," that is a culture and workflow-integration problem.

Run that on five or ten incidents and you will have your answer more reliably than any survey.

A second diagnostic is a cold-start usability test: ask a team member who didn't write the docs to find the answer to a specific question using only the documentation, while you watch silently. Don't help. Time them. Note where they get confused or give up. This surfaces whether "well-organized and accurate" is true from a reader's perspective, or only from the author's. These often diverge significantly — what feels organized to someone who knows the answer is frequently unnavigable to someone who doesn't.

The structural tells to watch for:
- If people are pointed directly to a doc link in Slack and still don't read it before replying with follow-up questions, that is culture — the doc was accessible and they still skipped it.
- If people try the docs but search fails them, or they land on the right page and still can't find the answer, that is an information architecture and writing problem.
- If the docs are rarely opened even during onboarding, when new people have the strongest incentive to self-serve, that is a trust and integration problem — people learned early that asking is more reliable.

The culture fix (if needed) is primarily social: every time a question is answered that has a doc, respond with the link before or alongside the answer, consistently, without shaming. After a few weeks of that, if volume drops, the docs were fine and the habit just hadn't formed. If volume stays flat despite everyone being pointed to docs regularly, either the docs aren't actually solving the user's need when they get there, or the workflow cost of leaving Slack to read them is too high.

The writing fix (if needed) is integration and discoverability, not reorganization: contextual links from Slack, from the codebase, from onboarding checklists; better search titles; shorter answers at the top with detail below. The problem with most internal docs that go unread is not that they lack information, it is that they require the reader to already know what to look for.

## Dissent worth keeping

Calling this a culture problem lets the docs off the hook. Nobody has a culture problem reading Stack Overflow answers that directly solve their problem. The bar for internal docs is just that they are lower quality than the alternatives — less reliable, harder to navigate, requiring more effort for the same outcome — so people rationally route around them. "Well-organized and accurate" is the author's assessment, not the user's, and those almost never agree. Fix the product before blaming the users.

## Confidence

Medium — the five perspectives agreed strongly on the diagnostic method and on the false-binary framing, but diverged meaningfully on whether writing quality or culture deserves more weight as the root cause, which reflects a genuine empirical uncertainty that only your specific team's data can resolve.
"""

Response Z:
"""
## Answer

The framing of "writing problem vs. culture problem" is a false binary, and it is probably the wrong question. The more useful reframe is this: your goal should not be "people read docs" — it should be "people get accurate answers quickly without creating human bottlenecks." Once you hold that goal instead, the writing-vs-culture distinction becomes a diagnostic question, not a moral one, and the answer changes what you fix.

Before doing anything else, run a cheap two-week diagnostic to actually separate the two failure modes. For every question that a doc should have answered, record the exact phrasing the person used when they asked it. Then take a colleague who did not write the doc — someone who would count as a naive reader — and give them only that phrasing. Ask them to find the relevant section in under 90 seconds using whatever the team normally uses to search. Alternatively, when someone asks a doc-answerable question in chat, reply by asking them to first find and post the relevant doc section along with a note on what it did not answer. Both approaches give you the same clean fork: if people cannot find the answer, you have a writing and information-architecture problem; if they find it readily but did not look in the first place, you have a culture and habit problem.

The tells for each failure mode are distinct. A writing and discoverability problem shows up as: the naive colleague searches using the asker's phrasing and lands on the wrong page, or lands nowhere, or lands in the right document but cannot locate the relevant section within 90 seconds. This is the author-illusion — "well-organized and accurate" is a judgment the author makes relative to how they think about the material, not relative to how a stressed colleague with a half-formed question arrives at it cold. The fix here is not prose polish. It is search, navigation, and cold-start usability: better titles that match the language people actually use when confused, shorter entry points, and anchor links that get someone to the answer without requiring them to read the whole document first.

A culture and habit problem shows up as: the naive colleague finds the section easily, but the original asker never tried. Asking a colleague is faster, warmer, status-safe, and carries no risk of looking foolish for misreading something. That calculus does not change when the prose gets cleaner. The fix is to reduce the cost of the right path, not to increase the virtue required to walk it. The most durable structural intervention is to wire your documentation into the channel where questions get asked — a bot that surfaces the relevant section before a human can respond removes the social shortcut without requiring anyone to change their habits voluntarily. Leaders modeling the behavior matters too: when someone senior publicly redirects a question to a doc, links to it, and visibly submits a correction when something is out of date, it signals that consulting docs is normal rather than pedantic. Reward the people who update docs, not just the people who write them.

One risk cuts across both failure modes and deserves special attention: staleness and trust. A single experience of following a doc to a wrong answer — acting on outdated information — can anchor avoidance behavior for months or years. If your team's unspoken prior is "the docs might be wrong," no amount of discoverability or nudging will overcome it. Part of your diagnostic should include whether people say things like "I wasn't sure if it was current" — that is its own signal, and the fix is a visible freshness indicator and a lightweight norm around flagging stale content, not more writing.

Confidence: high that the diagnostic approach correctly separates the two failure modes; medium that any single fix is sufficient, because most teams with unread docs have both problems layered on each other and need to work both sides simultaneously.

## Dissent worth keeping

The premise of the question conceals a failure of diagnosis in the other direction: "nobody reads the docs" may be evidence of a rational system functioning correctly, not a dysfunction that needs correction. Asking a colleague takes thirty seconds and returns a contextualized, personalized, uncertainty-flagged answer from someone who knows the team's current situation. Reading a doc takes five minutes and may still leave a gap that requires a follow-up question anyway. The equilibrium in which people ask rather than read is the market-clearing price of synchronous human knowledge transfer simply beating asynchronous written transfer on the dimensions that matter to the person with the question — speed, confidence, and social safety.

The right response to this is not to make the docs better or to nudge behavior. It is to stop fighting the equilibrium. Audit the existing documentation honestly: how many of those documents have ever changed a decision, prevented a repeated mistake, or served a reader who could not have gotten the answer from a person? Archive everything that fails that test. For the survivors, stop treating the doc as the primary artifact that people are supposed to consult proactively. Instead, make the doc the exhaust of the question-answering culture: when someone answers a repeated question in chat, that answer lives in the channel where everyone can see it, and the doc is linked as the receipts — the auditable, correctable record of what was said. Docs become downstream of conversation, not upstream of it. The question-answering behavior the team already has becomes the engine that keeps documentation alive and current, rather than a competing behavior that documentation is constantly losing to.
"""
