## Answer

Take the senior IC offer. The convergence across multiple independent angles here is unusually clean, and the reasoning behind it is more rigorous than a simple "do what you enjoy" argument.

The word "should" is doing dangerous work in your framing. "I feel like I should be more strategic" is not a goal — it is status anxiety wearing strategic clothes. Before you can evaluate which option is actually more strategic, you need to spend two hours writing down concretely what a good outcome at 35 looks like for you: compensation band, type of work day-to-day, autonomy level, optionality preserved. Do that first. Once you have it, test both offers against those criteria. You will almost certainly find that "strategic" maps far more naturally onto the IC path than you are currently giving it credit for.

The reversibility asymmetry alone should anchor your decision. IC-to-manager is moderately reversible at most companies — you can demonstrate people interest, take on mentoring, and make a credible transition in 2-3 years with technical credibility still intact. Manager-to-IC is genuinely hard: technical skills decay measurably within 18 months of leaving hands-on work, the market reprices you as a manager rather than an engineer, and the identity shift makes re-entry psychologically costly. If you are uncertain, preserving optionality has asymmetric value here. The IC offer lets you gather data on whether management appeals from a position of strength; the manager track bets heavily on a hypothesis you have not tested.

The base rates on "reluctant managers" are bad. People who enter management because they felt they *should* at 32 split roughly evenly into those who discover they love it and those who spend three years doing mediocre work and then claw back. The ones who thrive in management were *pulled* toward it — by genuine interest in developing people, by finding organizational dynamics energizing rather than draining, by wanting to operate through others. "I like coding more than meetings" is a signal about where your nervous system finds flow. That signal is data, not weakness.

One genuine risk on the IC side: check the career ladder at the smaller company before you sign. Talk to two or three senior ICs there who are 3-5 years ahead of you. Does anyone reach staff or principal? Is there a real architecture/systems ownership path, or does the ladder flatten at senior? If the IC ceiling is shallow, optionality flips — you would be choosing comfort over growth after all. But that is a due-diligence question with a discoverable answer, not a reason to default to management.

Concrete next steps: (1) write the "good outcome at 35" paragraph tonight; (2) schedule 30-minute calls with two senior ICs at the target company; (3) if both checks pass, take the offer. The decision is not close once you interrogate the premise.

Confidence: High on the senior IC direction; moderate on the specific timeline risks, which depend on that smaller company's ladder quality.
