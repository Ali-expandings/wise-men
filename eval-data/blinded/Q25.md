QUESTION:
"""
Why do psychology replication studies so often fail even for effects that seemed very robust? Is this just p-hacking retroactively exposed, or is there something deeper about how social science knowledge works?
"""

====================================================================
Response X:
"""
## Answer

The failures are overdetermined — multiple mechanisms pile on, and disentangling them matters for what the fix actually is.

**The statistical story is real but incomplete.** Researcher degrees of freedom (Gelman & Loken's "garden of forking paths") mean that even without conscious fraud, the many implicit decisions during analysis — outlier handling, covariate selection, when to stop collecting data, which of several DVs to report — can push null effects past p<0.05. Add publication bias (p-curve analyses show suspicious clustering just below 0.05 across entire literatures), chronically underpowered studies (median power in social psychology for small effects ~35%), and you get Ioannidis's result: most single-study p-values in the literature are false positives by construction. Pre-registration of studies before data collection substantially increases replication rates — this directly implicates undisclosed flexibility.

**But the pattern of failure is not random, which points to something deeper.** Hard subfields — experimental cognitive psychology, psychophysics, developmental — replicate well. Social priming, ego depletion, power poses, and many social cognition effects fail badly. If the problem were purely statistical practice, you'd expect more uniform failure. The differential tracks theoretical solidity.

**The deeper issue is construct validity and the stability of the phenomenon itself.** "Ego depletion" is not a natural kind — it's operationalized in dozens of different ways. When a replication changes the manipulation, it's arguably measuring something different, and the theoretical framework offers no principled way to predict which operationalizations should work. More fundamentally, human behavior is constitutively social and contextual: effects can be genuine in the original lab, with that population, at that cultural moment, and genuinely absent elsewhere. This isn't measurement error — it's that many psychological phenomena don't have the kind of cross-contextual stability that physical constants do. Gergen made this argument in 1973 and the replication crisis is empirical confirmation.

**The sociology of science compounds both.** Smaldino & McElreath's "natural selection of bad science" model shows that academic incentives — novelty premium, publish-or-perish, career advancement through surprising findings — systematically select for studies that are striking but fragile. The institution was optimized for the wrong fitness function.

So the full explanation is layered: (1) researcher degrees of freedom + publication bias inflated the literature with false positives; (2) winner's curse / selection effects inflated effect sizes even for real findings — replications regress to the true (smaller) mean; (3) constructs are often too vague to predict which operationalizations will work — enabling infinite post-hoc boundary-condition explanations after failure; (4) some effects are genuinely context-sensitive and the original studies didn't recognize the specific conditions as boundary conditions. The good news is that pre-registration, registered reports, open data, and larger samples are measurably improving reproducibility.

## Dissent worth keeping

Gilbert et al.'s rebuttal to the OSC 2015 paper is underrated: treating "replication" as binary is itself methodologically wrong. Some "failures" are actually successful replications of a smaller, real effect. And some apparent failures may reflect genuine moderators rather than false positives. The 39% replication rate figure is probably too pessimistic for the field as a whole — it oversamples social priming and high-novelty findings. Dismissing whole research programs because a specific operationalization didn't generalize is itself a logical error. The theorist's point also cuts against easy fixes: if phenomena are inherently context-sensitive, pre-registration and larger N won't solve it — you need better theory first, which is harder than statistical reform.

## Confidence

Moderate-high on the multi-causal picture (statistical + construct + contextual); the evidence base is solid. Lower on relative weighting — whether statistical reform alone is sufficient vs. whether fundamental theoretical reconstruction is needed. The pre-registration evidence leans methodological, but the differential subfield failure rate leans theoretical. Genuinely unresolved.
"""

Response Y:
"""
The replication crisis has multiple overlapping causes, and p-hacking is genuinely only part of the story — arguably not even the main part.

Start with the statistical layer. The original literature was built in an era where a p < .05 result in a single study of 40 undergrads was considered publishable evidence of a real effect. Publication bias did the rest: journals printed the hits, file drawers absorbed the misses, so the literature accumulated a curated sample of flukes presented as discoveries. P-hacking, HARKing (hypothesizing after results are known), and flexible stopping rules amplified this. When you then run a pre-registered replication with 10x the sample size, you're often discovering that the original study found noise dressed as signal. That part really is a methodological failure, and it's fixable.

But there's a second, deeper problem that has nothing to do with fraud or sloppiness: psychological effects are often genuine but fragile in ways that physical constants are not. An effect may be real in the original population, context, and moment — and simply not generalize. Priming studies are the canonical example. Social priming (walking slowly after thinking about old people, or becoming more creative after imagining being a professor) likely does produce small real effects under narrow conditions. But those conditions include the particular lab culture, demand characteristics of the specific experimenter, the sample's cultural background, the precise wording of instructions. When another lab in Germany runs the study on a different population with a slightly different cover story, the effect evaporates — not because the original was faked, but because the phenomenon was never the stable, context-independent mechanism it was written up as. This is called the "hidden moderator" problem, and social psychology is riddled with it.

The third problem is conceptual. Many classic effects were named and theorized at a high level of abstraction ("ego depletion," "stereotype threat," "facial feedback") but operationalized through a single paradigm. When the construct is vague and the measurement is narrow, you can't tell whether a failed replication means the theory is wrong, the operationalization was wrong, or the moderators changed. This is a theory-measurement mismatch that physics largely solved in the 17th century by building tight loops between theoretical constructs and operational definitions. Psychology never fully solved it, partly because the subject matter is genuinely harder.

There's also an underappreciated ecological problem: humans are culturally embedded in ways that make them a moving target. An effect measured on American undergraduates in 2003 involves people who have grown up with specific media, specific norms about authority and gender, specific ambient levels of stress. Run the same study in 2024 and those background conditions have shifted. This isn't unique to psychology — epidemiology has the same problem with changing exposures — but psychology often doesn't acknowledge it in write-ups.

The most honest framing is probably this: the replication crisis exposed that psychology was simultaneously doing three things that look alike but aren't — discovering robust mechanisms, finding real-but-fragile context-dependent phenomena, and generating statistical artifacts. The literature conflated all three into a single currency of "effects." Replication studies are essentially a triage process, sorting the durable from the fragile from the illusory. The failure rate looks shocking partly because the sorting was never done the first time around.

What's genuinely encouraging is that the field has responded better than many expected. Pre-registration, larger samples, adversarial collaborations, registered reports (where journals commit to publish regardless of results) are all meaningful reforms. The effects that survive this scrutiny — like classical conditioning, many memory phenomena, threat-based cognition — tend to be ones grounded in tighter mechanistic theory or tied to biological substrates that don't shift with cultural context. That's probably not a coincidence.
"""

"""
## Answer

Both mechanisms are real, both large, and they multiply — not add. The headline answer to the first question is that replication failures have two compounding engines: (1) quantitative research practice artifacts that inflated estimated effect sizes, and (2) genuine context-sensitivity that was never tested or acknowledged. Neither alone fully explains the record. The headline answer to the second question is that p-hacking and publication bias are substantial, documentable, and explain most of the *magnitude inflation* — but directional failures, moderator explosions, and the differential survival pattern across domains point to something structurally deeper about how social-science knowledge has been constructed.

The data establish multi-causality. OSC (2015): roughly 36% of 100 studies replicated; median replication effect was about half the original. Many Labs 2 found that between-lab variation explained almost nothing — the effect itself was unstable. The most telling pattern is *which* effects survive: low-level perception and cognition effects replicate at substantially higher rates than higher-order social effects (priming, ego depletion, power pose, social contagion). Ego depletion went from d ≈ 0.62 to indistinguishable from zero in a pre-registered N-in-the-thousands replication. A uniform statistics-cleaning story cannot explain this differential — if it were purely p-hacking, you would expect more uniform failure across domains. The fact that "closer to the neuroscience" effects survive and "closer to policy" effects collapse is itself a signal about construct quality and effect localization.

The mechanism by which even *honest, hard-working* researchers produced inflated estimates is mathematical, not moral. When a true effect is small and a study is underpowered (historical social-psych N ≈ 50–100/cell; median power estimated at 8–31%), the winner's curse operates: only large, noisy estimates of the effect clear the p < .05 bar and get published. The observed effect size is inflated by roughly the inverse of power — that is not fraud, it is a censored-sample artifact baked into the publication filter. A properly powered replication (d = .30 requires ≈ 234/group; legacy studies ran 30–40/group) will regress toward the true, smaller value, and that looks like "failure" even when the underlying phenomenon is real. This mechanism alone predicts the observed ≈ 50% effect-size shrinkage almost exactly.

But even cleaning up the statistics does not resolve everything, because a failed replication refutes the *conjunction* (theory + operationalization + sample assumptions + historical moment) — not the theory alone. This is the Duhem-Quine problem applied to psychology. A four-part decomposition is more useful than the binary "did it replicate": (a) *Measurement failure* — the operationalization stopped tracking the construct; (b) *Population failure* — the replication sample differs from the original in construct-relevant ways; (c) *Temporal failure* — the phenomenon itself changed in the world; (d) *Theoretical failure* — the mechanism never existed, the original was a false positive. Pre-registration and power reform directly fix only the fourth type. The first three require different remedies: measurement-invariance testing, pre-specified sampling criteria, and multi-cohort longitudinal designs respectively. Most reform discourse conflates all four, which is why the policy prescriptions feel simultaneously obviously correct and somehow insufficient.

What the field can do now, per effect: run a structured decomposition — effect-size deflation audit (PET-PEESE or selection-model correction), construct-invariance check across operationalizations, boundary-condition mapping via registered reports with pre-specified moderator variation, and theory accountability in the form of requiring at least one novel moderator prediction before publication. The surviving effects — and there are genuine ones — will look smaller, more localized, and more qualified than the literature currently represents. That is not nihilism; that is accurate cartography.

## Dissent worth keeping

**The non-stationarity objection:** Even after perfect statistical reform — full pre-registration, powered samples, open data — a significant fraction of social-science replications will continue to fail, because many social phenomena are genuinely non-stationary. Their magnitude, direction, and sometimes existence are constitutively shaped by population composition, cultural context, and historical moment. Replicating a 1985 study on authority and conformity on a 2025 WEIRD undergraduate cohort is not the same experiment run more cleanly — it is a *different experiment on a different population in a different world*. The WEIRD critique understated this: the problem is not just geographic sampling bias, but temporal non-equivalence of the psychological object itself. Stereotype threat, relative deprivation, the meaning of "willpower," the phenomenology of social exclusion — these are not fixed targets that a better-powered study will nail down. The real epistemological repair is not just cleaner statistics but a different representational convention: report findings as "effect X, magnitude Y, in population Z, measured during period T, under conditions C" — the way epidemiology and ecology index results by time, place, and environmental covariates — and fund multi-site, multi-cohort studies designed to *measure and model heterogeneity* rather than to answer the binary replication question.

The self-caveat this view must carry: without pre-specified boundary conditions declared *in advance*, "this effect is context-dependent" collapses into an unfalsifiable post-hoc excuse for any failure. The moderator-explosion problem in social psychology — where every failed replication spawns a list of plausible moderators, none of which were predicted — is already this pathology in action. The non-stationarity argument is compelling as a research-design philosophy and dangerous as a rhetorical defense move. The difference is whether the boundaries are predicted before the data arrive.

## Confidence

High confidence on the two core mechanisms and their compounding structure — the mathematical winner's curse argument is essentially airtight, the empirical differential-survival pattern is well-documented, and the Duhem-Quine decomposition is analytically sound. Moderate confidence on the relative weighting of the four failure types for any *specific* effect, since post-hoc attribution of a replication failure to measurement vs. population vs. theoretical causes is itself contested and requires data (moderator studies, measurement-invariance tests) that usually do not exist.
"""
