QUESTION:
"""
I'm ghostwriting a memoir for someone who has a compelling story but their writing is stiff and formal. They want their voice to come through. How do I write in someone else's voice without flattening them into mine?
"""

====================================================================
Response X:
"""
## Answer

Ghostwriting a memoir well is one of the hardest things a writer can do, precisely because the craft has to disappear. The goal is not translation — it's possession. You're not rendering their story in better prose, you're learning to think and feel and remember the way they do, and then writing from inside that.

The single most important thing you can do is spend a lot of time listening before you write a single word. Record every conversation. Not just the formal interviews where they're trying to be articulate, but the unguarded moments — when they laugh at something, when they get frustrated, when they trail off because a memory ambushed them. The formal interviews will give you the content. The in-between moments will give you the voice. Pay particular attention to how they tell a story when they're not trying to tell a story well. When someone relaxes and just talks, their syntax, their rhythms, their go-to metaphors, their particular way of entering a scene — that's where the real voice lives.

You're looking for a specific set of things. How long are their sentences? Do they qualify a lot, or do they make declarative statements? Do they use humor as a deflection, or do they sit in weight? Do they start stories in the middle and circle back, or do they set up carefully before paying off? What words do they genuinely use and what words would feel foreign in their mouth? A person who says "it was rough" will never say "it was harrowing" — even if harrowing is the better word. The better word is wrong. You need their word.

Stiffness in writing usually comes from one of two places: either the person writes more carefully than they speak, performing literacy instead of communicating, or they've been taught that good writing is formal writing and they're following rules that were never meant for memoir. Your job is to get underneath those habits. One technique is to ask them to tell you a story verbally first, then ask them to write it down, then compare. The gap between the spoken version and the written version is the gap you're trying to close. In the spoken version, you'll usually find more specificity, more mess, more life. The written version will be tidier and emptier.

Read the transcripts obsessively. Print them. Mark recurring words, recurring sentence structures, the specific way they move from one thought to another. You're building a grammar of their mind. Then when you sit down to write, don't start with the transcript — start by thinking yourself into their position. What did this feel like from inside? Where were they standing? What were they worried about, what did they want, what were they afraid to say? Write from that place, in that grammar, and then check yourself against the transcript to make sure you haven't drifted.

The flattening you're worried about happens when you start editing for quality before you've fully inhabited the voice. Your instinct as a writer is to make things better — tighter, more resonant, more controlled. But "better" by your standards may be "flatter" by theirs. The test is not whether the prose sounds good to you. The test is whether, when they read it, they say "yes, that's exactly right" rather than "yes, that's very nice." There's a difference. Very nice means you've gentrified their story. Exactly right means you've found them on the page.

One practical habit: when you finish a draft of a section, read it out loud and ask yourself whether you can hear them. Not whether it sounds like good memoir prose in the abstract, but whether it sounds like this specific person on their best day. If you can't quite hear them, look at the sentences. Are they too long? Too controlled? Have you removed all the false starts and repetitions that give a voice its texture? Some of what looks like mess in spoken language is actually structure — the way someone circles back to a detail reveals what they value, even if it's not rhetorically efficient.

The other thing to watch for is your own vocabulary creeping in. You'll find yourself reaching for words and constructions that you love but that they would never use. Mark those. Replace them. This is not about writing worse — it's about writing true to a different sensibility. Restraint in this context is a form of discipline, not a compromise.

Finally, share drafts early and often, and pay close attention to what they push back on. Not just the factual corrections, but the moments where they say "I wouldn't put it that way." Those moments are gold. Ask them how they would put it. That exchange — your draft, their correction — is the actual process of finding the voice. You're not going to nail it from transcripts alone. It's iterative. Each round of their pushback sharpens your ear until eventually you're writing things they read and say: I couldn't have said it better myself. Which, of course, is the whole point — they couldn't have said it better themselves, but it still sounds like them.
"""

## Answer

The first thing to understand is that your subject's stiffness is not their voice — it is their performance of what writing is supposed to sound like. The moment someone sits down to write, they begin impersonating an author, and the result is almost always a more formal, careful, and flattened version of how they actually think. The gap you are trying to close is not between their writing and yours; it is between their performed-writing-self and the self they become when they are mid-sentence explaining something to a friend and not thinking about prose at all.

The danger most ghostwriters underestimate is what one council member called double erasure: you suppress your own voice correctly, but then fill the space not with theirs but with a generic "good writing" voice — competent, clear, and belonging to no one. The subject reads it and feels it sounds dignified, and they may even approve it, which is the trap. The test is not "does this read well?" but "could this only have come from this specific person?" If the answer is not an immediate yes, you have substituted an idealized communicator for a real one.

The method the council converges on has four concrete stages.

First, run a dedicated voice-capture session that is entirely separate from your content interviews. Do not use this material in the book — its only purpose is to build your instrument. Record three to five hours of unstructured conversation, not interview questions but something closer to: tell me about the neighborhood you grew up in, tell me a time something went embarrassingly wrong, tell me about the last fight you had. Low-stakes stories work best. You are looking for the register they use when they trust the room. Supplement this with a prompt like "tell that again like you're explaining it to your brother" — the second telling almost always strips out the performative layer and shows you the real syntactic shape beneath.

Second, transcribe this material by hand, not by software, because the act of manual transcription forces you to hear things you would otherwise normalize away. Then build an annotated voice map — not a vocabulary list, but a record of rhetorical behaviors and syntactic fingerprints. You are looking for: how long their sentences run before they land on the point; whether they build toward a point or state it first and then explain; where in a sentence they place emotional weight; how they signal that something was hard without saying "it was hard"; what details they always reach for under pressure (physical sensation? other people's faces? economic facts?); how their clause-order shifts when they switch from storytelling to explanation; what they consistently omit because they assume it without stating it. You are also looking at their tolerance for grammatical incompleteness — some people think in fragments and qualifications, and prose that is always complete will ring false for them. Keep this voice map as a one-page reference document open in every drafting session.

Third, draft against that map the way a copyeditor works against a style sheet. For each passage, the question is not "is this good writing?" but "would they say it this way?" You are auditing the underlying syntactic logic, not just swapping vocabulary. The most common failure here is what could be called averaging-out: you borrow their phrases as seasoning on prose that is structurally yours. A sentence can contain all their words and still be built in your clause-order, which means the whole thing is still you.

Fourth, before any editor sees a chapter, it must go through a mandatory read-aloud gate. The subject reads it aloud themselves, not to you performing it but to themselves, following the text. Every place they stumble, hesitate, or self-correct without being prompted is a voice break. The mouth catches unnatural rhythm that the eye skips over entirely. This stumble is your ground truth. It is more reliable than their editorial notes, and considerably more reliable than their approval.

The key risks to name explicitly. Voice creep under deadline is real: the path of least resistance always runs toward your own clause-order because it is fluent and fast. Audit for it deliberately on revision, not in the first draft. Over-correction in the other direction can produce performed-casual, which is just a different costume and not their actual skin — the goal is not to reproduce their speech but to find the third register between their stiff writing-self and their literal transcribed speech. Editors and fact-checkers, when they come, will sand down idiosyncrasies because idiosyncrasy looks like error to someone reading for correctness — brief them explicitly on what is intentional. And most importantly: if the subject reads a chapter and approves it quickly and with relief, and tells you how much better it sounds than they expected, treat that as a yellow flag, not a green one. The version they find most comfortable to approve may be the version in which their real voice has been most thoroughly replaced.

Confidence: high. There is strong method consensus across four of five members, independent convergence on recorded unguarded speech as the source material, and the annotated-behavior voice map plus read-aloud validation as the two most reliable quality gates. The core diagnostic — stiffness as performance, double erasure as the primary failure mode — is well-grounded and actionable.

## Dissent worth keeping

The entire framing of this question contains a category error, and four out of five answers here miss it. When a stiff person tells you they want their voice to come through, they are not asking you to reproduce their stiffness. They are asking you to realize the warmer, funnier, more vivid communicator they already believe themselves to be. The job is not mimicry of output — it is fulfillment of intent.

Stop mining surface features. Cadence, clause-order, pet phrases — these are the furniture of a voice, not the voice itself. What you should be mining is emotional logic: what they find genuinely funny versus what they perform as funny; where they go quiet because something matters too much to frame correctly; what they consistently understate because full statement would feel like a loss of control. Once you have that emotional logic, your job is to construct a voice that executes it at a higher register than they currently command. Not their voice as it exists — their voice as it was trying to become.

The practical test for this is not the read-aloud stumble, which will reliably flag the places where the prose sounds most alive, because alive writing is slightly unfamiliar. The practical test is their physical reaction when you hand them a chapter before they have time to editorialize. Watch their face. Their notes will ask you to sand down the vivid parts — that is almost universal — but their face in the first few seconds tells you what is actually true. Trust the face. The notes are what they think they should want; the face is what they actually felt.

Yes, this approach risks constructing a voice that diverges far enough from their actual output that they feel like an impostor in their own book. That is a real risk and it should be named honestly. But the mimicry approach carries the opposite risk: faithfully reproducing their tics can read less like a memoir and more like a deposition. Some people are stiff all the way down, and a perfect transcription of that stiffness is not their voice — it is their limitation. The question is which risk you are more willing to take. "Realize who they were trying to be" and "copy who they are on the page" are not the same job, and this council should not pretend they are.

"""
## Answer

The first mistake is treating voice as a list of vocabulary words. Voice is a five-layer system: word choice, sentence syntax, rhythmic pacing, emotional register, and what the narrator notices versus ignores. Missing even one layer produces a book that sounds almost like the person but not quite — uncanny valley prose. Before writing a word of the memoir, you need to build a working model of all five.

The raw material is recordings, not drafts. Interview the subject extensively, on subjects that matter to them emotionally, and record everything. Then transcribe and mine the transcripts not for content but for pattern: How long are their sentences before they interrupt themselves? Do they use qualifiers compulsively ("I think," "maybe," "sort of") or do they assert flatly? What specific words do they return to? Where do they go quiet or shift register? What do they always omit when telling a story — those omissions are as much part of voice as what they include. Build a voice glossary of 20-30 characteristic expressions and structural habits from the transcripts. This becomes your reference artifact for the whole project.

The next threat is your own aesthetics leaking in under deadline pressure. This happens even to careful ghosts, especially at the syntactic level: the subject's words get stuffed into the ghost's sentence structures. The quality gate is reading drafts aloud, not silently. When a sentence sounds smooth and right to you but wrong when spoken, that's your rhythm, not theirs. Read every draft passage aloud before sending it, and have the subject read passages aloud to you during revision. If they stumble, that's the voice breaking.

One underrated element: trust. The subject's stiffness on the page is almost certainly anxiety-driven — they're performing writing rather than speaking. Your job before the structural work is to make them feel safe enough to be unguarded in conversation. The vivid, specific, idiosyncratic version of their voice only surfaces when they stop managing how they're perceived. The ghost who earns that trust gets a fundamentally different quality of raw material than the one who doesn't.

Finally, accept that you are not a tape recorder. You are building the best version of their voice — the voice they have when they're at their most themselves, distilled. This involves editorial judgment about which version of them is most true. That is a creative act, not just a transcription act. The goal is not to flatten yourself into them but to make yourself temporarily permeable to them, then write.

## Dissent worth keeping

The Devil's Advocate position: "The client's real problem isn't that they need their voice captured — it's that they don't actually know what their voice is. Most people's mental image of their own voice is aspirational, not descriptive. The ghost who chases 'authenticity' too literally produces books that feel authentic to the subject and alienating to readers. Great ghostwriting is often invention, not mimicry. You shouldn't suppress your own instrument — you should channel the subject through it."

## Confidence

Medium — the practical techniques had strong agreement across perspectives, but there is genuine and unresolved tension between the "capture their voice faithfully" framing and the "construct the best version of their voice" framing, which affects how the ghost should handle moments where the subject's actual patterns would read as flat on the page.
"""
