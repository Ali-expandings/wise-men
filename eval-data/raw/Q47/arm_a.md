Yes, and this is one of the most practically important tensions in nonprofit management. Here's how to think through it clearly.

The core question is whether the restricted funding is fungible in practice. If you have other unrestricted funds you would have spent on this program anyway, then accepting restricted funding for it frees up those unrestricted funds for your preferred programs — the donor's restriction becomes meaningless in net terms. This is the first thing to check before agonizing over anything else. Many nonprofits solve the problem this way without any ethical tension at all.

If the program genuinely wouldn't happen without the restricted gift, the calculus changes. Now you have to ask: does running this less-effective program actually cost you anything, or does it just produce less good than your preferred alternatives? The relevant comparison is not "this program vs. my best program" — it's "this program vs. nothing," because the donor's money won't follow you to your preferred programs if you decline. If the program still clears your minimum bar for doing net good, then accepting the money is defensible and probably correct.

Where it gets genuinely hard is if the program is not just less effective but actively competes with your better programs — for staff time, organizational attention, relationships, or reputational positioning. Restricted funding that looks like a gift on paper can be a net cost if it crowds out better work or distorts your organization's mission over time. This is the "funding that pulls you off-mission" problem, and it's real. The discipline is to calculate that hidden cost honestly rather than letting the dollar amount blind you to it.

There's also a relationship and integrity dimension. If you accept the gift but privately consider the funded program second-rate, you are making an implicit promise to the donor that you don't fully mean. Some nonprofit leaders handle this by being frank with donors: "We'll run this program because you value it, but I want you to know we think Program X produces more impact per dollar — here's why." Some donors appreciate that honesty and it can redirect their future giving. Silently taking the money while thinking the donor is wrong about program effectiveness is a small erosion of trust that compounds over time.

The principled framework, put simply: accept if the program clears a minimum threshold of doing genuine good, the funding is truly additional rather than crowding out better work, and you can represent it to the donor honestly. Decline — or negotiate toward less restricted funding — if the program fails that threshold, if the hidden costs are real, or if accepting would require you to be something less than straight with the donor about your own beliefs.
