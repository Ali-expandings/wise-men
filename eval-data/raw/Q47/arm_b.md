## Answer

There is a principled framework, and it runs through five sequential tests rather than a single calculation.

**1. Mission threshold first.** Is the program within your mission at all? If it's genuinely off-mission, no dollar amount justifies acceptance — the organizational and reputational costs compound over time. If it's within mission but less effective, proceed to the next test.

**2. Run honest fungibility math.** The common argument for accepting restricted gifts is that money is fungible — their dollars fund Program B, your existing dollars flow to Program A. This is sometimes true but often overstated. Ask specifically: does this gift displace unrestricted dollars you'd have spent anyway, or does it add net capacity? Factor in compliance costs, reporting burden, and staff attention — these are not fungible. If the net effect is genuinely additive, the case for accepting strengthens.

**3. Negotiate scope before deciding.** This step is underused. Most major donors are more flexible than their initial ask implies, particularly if you frame the conversation around evidence and shared mission. You can often propose a modified version — a pilot, a narrower focus, an integrated approach — that partially satisfies the donor's intent while shifting allocation toward your preferred design. Do this before deciding accept/decline.

**4. Assess relationship trajectory.** One restricted gift from a donor on a path to unrestricted support is qualitatively different from a donor who will only ever give with strings. If this gift sets a pattern, your estimate of long-run cost goes up substantially.

**5. Set a floor based on harm, not just suboptimality.** "Less effective" and "harmful" are not the same threshold. Running a merely suboptimal program with restricted funds may be defensible. Running a program that actively harms beneficiaries, misleads donors, or crowds your most effective work off the portfolio is not — regardless of what the fungibility math says.

The synthesis: accept if the program clears the mission bar, the fungibility math is genuinely positive after honest accounting, and you've exhausted negotiation. Decline if it fails any of those tests, or if accepting would ratify a donor-control pattern you can't sustain.

## Dissent worth keeping

You're operating on the premise that you know which programs are more effective, and the donor is simply wrong. But the donor is a stakeholder with their own values and information, and pluralism in funding allocation has independent value — a world where every dollar flows to the current highest-ranked intervention is not obviously optimal. Before concluding you should accept their money and privately minimize their program, ask whether your effectiveness judgment is well-calibrated and whether this is a genuine difference in values rather than a mistake on their part. The adversarial implicit contract — "I'll take your money but I know better" — is corrosive if it becomes the actual operating relationship.
