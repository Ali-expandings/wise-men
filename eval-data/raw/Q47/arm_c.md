## Council answer

The fungibility argument most nonprofit leaders reach for first is largely a fig leaf. The math looks clean on paper — $100k restricted to Program B "frees up" $100k for Program A — but in practice it frees up whatever marginal slice wasn't already committed, minus the overhead you now owe to administer, report on, staff, and maintain Program B in perpetuity. The real danger is not the Year 1 calculation. It is the path-dependent capture trajectory: Year 1 you accept; Year 2 the donor sees their program thriving and increases the restricted gift to 40% of your budget; you have hired staff for Program B; you cannot kill it without layoffs and a donor crisis; by Year 3 your public identity, board composition, and grant eligibility (funders pattern-match to what you already do) have all drifted toward Program B. The donor did not need to take over — you handed them the wheel incrementally, one fungibility calculation at a time. This is the standard capture trajectory for small nonprofits accepting major restricted gifts from a single donor, and no monitoring protocol catches it because each individual step looks defensible.

That is the reframe the council leads with, because the practical question is not "does accepting produce positive expected value?" but "am I about to start a path I cannot reverse?"

**The actionable synthesis, in order:**

**1. Negotiate before you decide anything.** Offer the donor a "directed advisory" arrangement: their gift enters a general effectiveness pool, and you commit in writing to share annual impact data on the program they care about, including comparative outcome data across your portfolio. This costs you almost nothing if the donor genuinely wants impact. If they refuse — if they will only give if the dollars are earmarked to Program B — that refusal is information worth more than the gift. It tells you their goal is control, not impact. Walk away from funders who will not accept transparency-over-earmarks.

**2. Before accepting any restricted gift, run the honest conditional tests the rest of the council supplies:**

- *Deontologist / Virtue test:* Can you disclose your honest effectiveness assessment to the donor before accepting — plainly, not softened, not advocacy-dressed-as-conversation? Tell them you believe Program B is less cost-effective than Program A, that you would allocate differently with discretion, and that you are prepared to execute Program B faithfully if they still want to proceed. If you cannot bring yourself to have that conversation, the reluctance is diagnostic: you are already in a compromised posture.

- *Rights / beneficiary test:* Does Program B serve real beneficiaries meaningfully, and can you represent that honestly to the people it serves? The line that cannot be crossed: accepting funds for a program you believe nets harm, or one that crowds out capacity for more-vulnerable populations. "Less effective per dollar" is not automatically a harm — but name who loses when your best program loses capacity. Do not dissolve it into aggregate math.

- *Honest counterfactual test (Utilitarian, with eyes open):* Run the welfare numbers, but include the full cost — leadership attention, administration overhead, the capture risk, the opportunity cost of not scaling your best work. Accept only if the net is clearly positive after those costs, and document the reasoning so you can revisit it at Year 2.

**3. Set structural trip-wires now.** If you accept, cap the restricted gift as a percentage of total budget (20% is a reasonable ceiling for a single restricted donor), build a sunset clause or re-evaluation milestone into the grant agreement, and brief your board explicitly on the capture risk so they can hold you accountable to the ceiling.

## Dissent worth keeping

The conventional four-member position — run the principled framework, accept if it clears the tests — deserves its strongest form, because it is not obviously wrong.

The Utilitarian counterfactual has genuine force: if you decline, the donor's money does not follow you to your preferred programs. The comparison is not "Program B vs. Program A" — it is "Program B vs. nothing, with those dollars." If the donor would otherwise fund a worse organization, or not fund anything, your declining redirects real resources less efficiently. Real beneficiaries of Program B, who would have been served, now are not. The math may genuinely favor accepting even after accounting for overhead, and refusing on trajectory-risk grounds means imposing a certain cost on identifiable people today to hedge against a probabilistic organizational harm years from now.

The Rights Advocate sharpens this: "less effective" is not the same as harmful, and if the evidence base for your effectiveness hierarchy is thin or contested — which it usually is in nonprofit program evaluation — the donor's preference deserves more weight than the leader's internal ranking suggests. Some beneficiaries served, by a program that is merely second-best, beats none served. You are a fiduciary to beneficiaries first, and that cuts toward accepting restricted funding for programs that still serve them, not just against it.

The Deontologist adds that donor autonomy is not nothing: a major donor directing their own resources by their own values is a moral agent, not an obstacle, and reflexive resistance to restriction can itself become a posture that treats donors instrumentally.

The flag: this council's unusual structure is that the contrarian view — restricted acceptance triggers path-dependent capture no monitoring catches — was rated the strongest position, and the conventional accept-if-tests-clear framework is the dissent. Readers who find the capture trajectory argument speculative or overstated should weight the conventional position accordingly; it is not a minority fringe view, it is the near-consensus of mainstream nonprofit governance advice.
