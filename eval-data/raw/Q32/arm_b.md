## Answer

Both failure modes — desperate and arrogant — come from the same root cause: the email is fundamentally about you, not about them. Desperate emails overexplain why you need the relationship; arrogant emails overstate what you bring. Both happen because the writer hasn't done enough research to write anything genuinely grounded in the recipient's situation.

The right register at VP level is "peer who noticed something specific." Not supplicant, not authority. Here is what that requires in practice:

**Do the research first, then write.** Before you write word one, you need to know: What is this VP's current stated priority? (LinkedIn posts, conference talks, company announcements.) What is their likely quarterly pressure right now? What is the specific gap or friction your partnership would address for *them*? If you can't answer these, your email will be generic, and generic at VP level reads either as desperate or arrogant depending on your tone.

**Open on them, not on you.** The first sentence should demonstrate that you know something specific about their world — not flattery ("I love what you're building"), but a specific observation ("Your Q1 announcement about X suggested you're moving into Y — we've been solving exactly the distribution problem that creates"). This is the fastest way to signal you're worth reading. Delete every sentence in your draft that begins with "I" or "We" until you are structurally forced to write about them first.

**Match your ask to your trust level, which is zero.** "Partnership" is a red-flag word because it signals a large, bilateral commitment from someone who has no track record with them. Ask for a 20-minute call to see if there's fit. Frame it as low-cost and easy to say yes to. The goal of the cold email is not to close the partnership — it is to earn 20 minutes.

**VP-specific register means outcomes at the quarterly level.** VPs are not CEOs (they don't live in vision-and-strategy abstraction) and they're not managers (they don't live in feature-level specifics). They live in: Are we hitting the number? What is blocking us? What cross-team friction is costing us? Your email should locate itself in that register — not "we do X," but "here's a specific thing that's probably friction on your plate right now."

**The subject line and first sentence are where the email is decided.** A perfect body paragraph does not save a generic subject line. The subject should be specific and slightly unexpected — not "Partnership Inquiry" but something that contains a real signal about what you know about their situation.

The mechanical fix for three drafts that aren't working: read each draft and highlight every sentence that is about you or your company. If more than 30% of the email is about you, rewrite until the first two-thirds is entirely about them, then make one crisp value claim, then make a small ask.
