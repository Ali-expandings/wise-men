## Council answer

Before you write draft four, stop and pressure-test the channel itself. The Devil's Advocate position — rated strongest by your own council — is that three failed drafts are not a copy problem; they are a diagnostic signal that you are pushing on a low-leverage instrument. Cold emails to VPs carry a 2–5% response base rate under ideal conditions, and "ideal conditions" almost always include some warm signal already present. Ask yourself honestly: is there anyone in your network, even two degrees out, who knows this person or works at that company? A contextual warm introduction converts at five to ten times the rate of cold outreach, and it arrives with relationship capital already attached. If a direct warm intro is impossible, look for a director-level champion inside the company — someone who has publicly written or spoken about a problem you solve — and build a small, genuine relationship first. Enter through them. The VP path opens from inside more reliably than from a cold subject line.

If you have genuinely exhausted warm paths and the cold email is your only real option, then the craft fix is this: the desperate/arrogant axis is not a tone problem, it is a subject-positioning and status-framing problem. Desperate emails make the sender the grammatical subject of every sentence ("I'd love to," "I've been following your work"). Arrogant emails treat the reader as a prop for the sender's agenda ("we'd be a great fit," "this is a unique opportunity"). Both failure modes are about you. The correct register is that of a peer who has done their homework and is proposing a specific, mutually useful trade — not an audition, not a pitch deck in paragraph form.

The structure that achieves this has four moves. First, open on one specific, verifiable thing the VP or their team has done recently: a product launch, a strategic announcement, a public interview, a hiring pattern. Not a compliment — a specific observation that implies a real business problem. Second, state in one sentence the concrete problem that observation implies and why you are relevant to it, with one real proof point (a result, a customer, a number). Third, make the smallest possible ask: a yes/no question ("Is this something your team is actively working on right now?"), not a "30-minute partnership call." A low-friction ask signals that you respect their time and are confident enough not to over-reach. Fourth, delete every sentence that is managing your own anxiety rather than serving their decision — this includes "I know you're busy," "sorry to reach out cold," and any sentence that begins with "I" in the first line. Keep the whole thing under 120 words.

One structural checkpoint before you send: kill your first sentence. In all three failing drafts it is almost certainly about you. The email should begin mid-thought, on them, on something real.

The only uncertainty is how clean your warm-path alternatives actually are — which is yours to assess, not ours.

## Dissent worth keeping

The synthesis above is too generous to the premise. You should not send this cold email at all — not even a perfectly written one.

Here is the argument in its hardest form: a cold email to a VP, regardless of how well-crafted, is a piece of sales collateral. VPs have assistants, filters, and pattern-recognition built over years precisely to identify and route sales collateral away from their attention. You are not going to out-write that system with better subject-positioning. The 2–5% response base rate is not a copy problem waiting to be optimized; it is the structural ceiling of the channel, and it exists because the channel is wrong for the relationship you are trying to build.

Three failed drafts do not mean you need a fourth draft. They mean you are rearranging deck chairs. Every draft you send through this channel is burning a name — this VP's name, in this context, on this approach — that you will not be able to re-approach cold again without the prior silence as context. You get one shot at a cold first impression, and spending it on even a good cold email is spending it on a 2–5% lottery ticket.

The only correct move is to stop writing and start mapping. Find the warm intro. Find the director-level champion. Find the person who posted on LinkedIn last month about the exact problem you solve and start a real conversation there. If none of those paths exist, the honest conclusion is that you do not yet have enough surface area with this company to justify a VP-level ask, and the right investment is building that surface area first — not polishing the email one more time. A perfectly written cold email to the wrong entry point is still the wrong entry point.
