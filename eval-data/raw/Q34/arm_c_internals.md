## MEMBER: Editor (sonnet-4.6)
==================================================================

## Core judgment

The central challenge in ghostwriting is that you are not translating their ideas into language — you are excavating the language that already lives in them and making it legible on the page. Stiffness in writing almost always means the person is performing a version of themselves they think writing requires, not speaking the way they actually think. Your job is to find the layer underneath the performance: the sentence rhythms, the pet phrases, the particular way they interrupt themselves or trail off or over-explain one specific type of thing. Voice capture is fundamentally an act of close listening before it is ever an act of writing.

## Top risks

- Averaging them out: you extract a handful of surface quirks (a few filler phrases, their preferred intensifiers) and apply them as seasoning to prose that is structurally yours, producing a hybrid that sounds like neither of you
- Over-correcting informality: knowing their writing is stiff, you lean colloquial and end up writing your version of their casual register, not theirs
- Transcription flatness: if you work from recorded interviews, you transcribe the words but miss the prosodic information — where they speed up, where they slow down and repeat, where their syntax breaks mid-thought because the emotion caught them
- Homogenizing their inconsistency: real voices have internal contradictions (formal in one context, loose in another, careful about one subject, reckless about another), and editing for consistency kills that texture
- Imposing your own sentence logic: specifically, your default for clause order, for how subordinate clauses relate to main clauses, and for how you handle transitions — these are deeply habitual and nearly invisible to the writer who has them

## Recommended change

Before drafting a single sentence, build a voice map from raw material — not from their writing, but from recordings. Conduct long interviews and transcribe them verbatim, including false starts, repairs, and repetitions. Then mine that transcription for four specific things: (1) their default clause-order pattern when telling a story versus when explaining something (these are often different), (2) the specific words and phrases they use when they are emotionally activated versus when they are being analytical — the vocabulary shifts between registers, (3) their tolerance for incompleteness — do they leave sentences grammatically unresolved when the meaning is clear, or do they always close the loop, and (4) what they omit, meaning which kinds of contextual information they assume without stating. Once you have those four profiles, use them as constraints when drafting: write a passage, then audit it against each constraint explicitly, the way a copyeditor marks against a style sheet. The goal is not to insert their phrases into your prose but to rebuild the underlying syntactic logic so that the prose generates their phrases naturally.

## Confidence

High, because this method is structural rather than cosmetic — it targets the level at which voice actually lives (syntax and assumption patterns) rather than vocabulary surface, which is where most voice-capture advice operates and where most attempts fail.

## Weakest assumption

The analysis assumes that recorded speech is a reliable proxy for the voice the subject wants to project in their memoir — but some people genuinely speak in a register they do not want on the page, and the voice they are reaching for in writing, however stiffly, may be closer to the version of themselves they actually intend to present to readers; in that case, the real work is not excavating their speech but helping them find the written register they are trying to inhabit and have not yet unlocked.

## MEMBER: Audience Advocate (sonnet-4.6)
==================================================================

## Core judgment

The central failure in ghostwriting is not that you impose your voice, but that you substitute a generic "good writing" voice for both your voice and theirs — a kind of double erasure. The subject needs to read the manuscript and feel that it captured how they actually think, not how they wish they sounded; the reader needs to feel a specific consciousness with quirks, gaps, and contradictions, not a smoothed-out authorized version. Your job is to become a mirror precise enough that the subject's idiosyncrasies pass through you intact, which requires disciplined self-suppression far more than creative projection.

## Top risks

- **You polish away the signal.** Stiff, formal writing often carries real information about the subject's personality — their caution, their formality as self-protection, their class background. If you make it "flow better," you may be deleting who they are.
- **You conflate their stated preferences with their actual voice.** They say they want their voice to come through, but what they often mean is that they want their best self rendered — which is a different, negotiated thing, not raw transcription.
- **You write your own emotional beats onto their material.** When a story beat moves you, you reach for your own language to honor it, and the subject's prose fingerprint disappears exactly where it most needs to be present.
- **The voice is consistent but flat.** You master their surface tics — word choice, sentence rhythm — but miss the deeper pattern: what they reliably avoid saying, where they deflect with humor, what they always over-explain.
- **The subject approves inauthenticity.** Subjects sometimes prefer the polished false version in review because it sounds more dignified, and you take that approval as validation when it is actually a warning sign.

## Recommended change

Before writing a single chapter, conduct a dedicated voice-capture session that is separate from your content interviews. Ask the subject to tell you two or three small, low-stakes stories — something that happened last week, a minor annoyance, a childhood ritual — and record them. Do not use those stories in the book. Use them purely as voice specimens. Transcribe them verbatim and then annotate specifically: Where do they slow down and repeat themselves? What category of detail do they always reach for (sensory, social, logistical)? How do they signal that something was hard without saying it was hard? What is their characteristic move when they are about to say something they half-believe? Build a private style sheet from those annotations — not a list of vocabulary words, but a list of rhetorical behaviors — and consult it every time you write a passage that feels too smooth or too much like you. The test for any finished paragraph is not "does this sound like them" but "could this only have come from them."

## Confidence

High — because this approach addresses the structural problem (voice capture is underprepared in most ghostwriting engagements) rather than offering a craft tip that treats the symptom.

## Weakest assumption

The analysis assumes that the subject's authentic voice is actually accessible through conversation and that it differs meaningfully from their stiff written voice — but in some cases, the stiffness is not a mask over a warmer oral voice; it is the voice, and the oral register is simply a different mode of the same fundamental self-presentation. If that is true, the entire voice-capture strategy still works, but the output will surprise the subject, and the negotiation about what "their voice" means will need to happen explicitly rather than being resolved by craft.

## MEMBER: Rhetorician (sonnet-4.6)
==================================================================

## Core judgment

Stiff and formal writing is almost never a person's voice — it is their performance of what they believe writing is supposed to sound like. The gap you are trying to close is not between their voice and your voice; it is between their performed writing self and their actual speaking self. Your job as ghostwriter is not to replicate either of those, but to discover the third thing: the voice they would use if they trusted the page the way they trust a room full of friends. That voice already exists in them. You are its transcriptionist, not its author.

## Top risks

- **Laundering their quirks out:** You hear an "incorrect" construction they use repeatedly — a habitual sentence rhythm, a colloquial word, a tendency to circle back — and your editorial instinct cleans it up. You have just removed the fingerprint.
- **Projecting your aesthetic:** The memoir starts sounding literary and poised in ways that feel like you, not them. The subject reads it and says "this is beautiful" but does not say "this sounds like me." Those are not the same compliment.
- **Over-correcting from stiff to performed casualness:** Trying to loosen the voice by injecting slang or truncated sentences can produce something equally false — a different costume, not skin.
- **Losing the idiolect under deadline pressure:** The longer a project runs and the more drafts accumulate, the more their distinctive patterns erode and your default patterns fill the space. Voice drift is cumulative and hard to notice in real time.
- **Confusing story with voice:** You can preserve their narrative choices faithfully while still writing every sentence in your cadence. These are separable problems and both require attention.

## Recommended change

Before writing a single sentence, conduct three to five hours of unstructured recorded conversation — not interview, conversation. Do not follow a topic list. Let them meander, interrupt themselves, use the wrong word and correct it, tell the same story twice with different emphases. Then transcribe those recordings yourself, by hand if possible, not with auto-transcription software. What you are looking for is not quotable material but syntactic fingerprints: how long their sentences run before they need to reset, what words they reach for under pressure, whether they build to a point or start with the point and then explain it, whether they hedge or assert, whether humor arrives as a twist at the end or as a throwaway in the middle. Make a one-page cheat sheet of these patterns — call it a voice map — and keep it open every time you draft. When a sentence feels too smooth, too clean, too fluent, check it against the map. The test is not "is this good writing" but "would they say it this way."

## Confidence

High — because this problem is structurally identical across every ghostwriting engagement regardless of subject matter, and the diagnosis (stiff writing as performance rather than authentic voice) is well-supported by what we know about writing anxiety and register code-switching.

## Weakest assumption

The analysis assumes that the subject's spoken voice is a reliable guide to their authentic written voice — but some people genuinely think and express themselves more naturally in writing than in speech, in which case the conversational recordings would surface a different kind of performance rather than the unguarded register you are hunting for.

## MEMBER: Strategist (sonnet-4.6)
==================================================================

## Core judgment

The central danger in ghostwriting a memoir is that the ghost's voice is already fluent and the subject's is not, which means the path of least resistance always runs toward the ghost. The only reliable antidote is to build the voice guide *before* you write a single sentence of prose — not from the subject's existing writing (which is stiff and therefore unrepresentative) but from hours of recorded, transcribed, unguarded speech. The whole workflow should be structured so that every draft cycle runs through the subject's ears before it runs through any editor's eyes, because the subject is the only instrument that can detect wrongness reliably.

## Top risks

- Harvesting voice from the wrong source: transcribing their formal emails or previous writing rather than their spoken, off-guard storytelling, which encodes the very stiffness you are trying to escape.
- Ghost fluency creep: your prose instincts are strong enough that a paragraph can slip into your register without either of you noticing, especially on a deadline.
- Approval drift: the subject begins approving prose that "sounds fine" rather than prose that sounds like them, because they start accommodating your style rather than defending their own.
- Loss of idiosyncrasy over revision: the phrases that feel weird or too informal get smoothed out by well-meaning editors or fact-checkers before the subject can defend them.
- Inconsistent calibration: the voice feels right in chapter two and flat by chapter nine because you are working faster, referencing your own ear more, and doing fewer read-back sessions.

## Recommended change

Set up a structured voice-harvesting process in the first two to three weeks before any prose is written. Record six to eight hours of conversation — not an interview in the journalistic sense but more like an oral storytelling session where you ask for the same story multiple times ("tell me that again but pretend you're telling it to your brother"). Transcribe everything, then go through the transcripts and mark every phrase, sentence, or construction that is distinctly *theirs*: unusual word order, a recurring metaphor, the way they hedge or don't hedge, what they call their parents, how they describe money or failure or joy. From that, build a two-to-three page voice sheet: not rules, but examples — real excerpts from the transcripts, annotated with what makes them characteristic. Use that sheet as a reference every time you sit down to write. Then build a mandatory read-aloud step into every feedback cycle: you send a chapter, the subject reads it aloud to themselves (not silently), and marks anywhere they stumble or feel a sentence is not something they would say. Silent reading is a poor filter because the eye skips; the mouth catches unnatural rhythm immediately. That read-aloud stumble is your ground truth.

## Confidence

High — this workflow recommendation is well-established across professional ghostwriting practice and is grounded in a straightforward principle: the subject's spoken voice is the data source, and their physical read-back experience is the validation instrument.

## Weakest assumption

The analysis assumes the subject is a fluid and natural speaker even if they are a stiff writer — that the spoken transcripts will actually yield the idiomatic, warm voice they want in the book. If the subject is equally guarded and formal in speech, or if their natural spoken register is genuinely not one they want preserved in print, the entire voice-harvesting method needs to be renegotiated and the goal shifts from capturing their actual voice to co-constructing an idealized one, which is a fundamentally different and more fraught project.

## MEMBER: Devil's Advocate (sonnet-4.6)
==================================================================

## Core judgment

The premise of the question contains a category error: a stiff, formal person who wants "their voice to come through" is not asking you to reproduce their stiffness — they are asking you to fulfill the self-image they have of themselves as a communicator, which is almost always more vivid, warmer, and more rhythmically alive than their actual prose. Your job is not mimicry of their output but realization of their intent. The subject's authentic voice is not what they write; it is what they mean, feel, and remember — and your editorial imagination is the instrument that makes that legible on the page.

## Top risks

- Mistaking fidelity for quality: faithfully reproducing their verbal tics, passive constructions, and hedging produces a book that reads like a legal deposition, which will embarrass them more than a ghostwriter's voice would
- Over-correcting into the opposite trap: inventing a voice so vivid it no longer feels like theirs, leaving them unable to read the manuscript aloud without feeling like an imposter at their own story
- Treating "their voice" as a fixed object to be captured rather than a latent possibility to be developed, which makes the collaboration static rather than generative
- Conflating interview style with narrative style: most people speak in sentence fragments and associative loops, which are charming in conversation and incoherent on the page

## Recommended change

Stop mining interviews for linguistic surface features — cadence, vocabulary, pet phrases — and start mining them for emotional logic: what does this person find funny, where do they hesitate, what do they clearly understate because it matters too much? Then construct a voice that executes that emotional logic at a higher register than they currently command. Show them a chapter, watch their physical reaction before they speak, and let that gut response (not their editorial notes) guide revision. Their notes will ask you to sand down the vivid parts; their face will tell you which parts are true.

## Confidence

Medium — this position holds strongly for subjects who are genuinely inarticulate writers but self-aware storytellers; it becomes riskier when the subject is a professional communicator who has a distinctive public style that readers already know and will fact-check against the book.

## Weakest assumption

The whole argument rests on the assumption that the subject's self-image of their voice is aspirationally warmer and more direct than their actual prose — but some stiff, formal people are stiff and formal all the way down, and their authentic voice really is the formal one, in which case constructing a "better" voice for them is simple fraud against the reader and a betrayal of the subject.
