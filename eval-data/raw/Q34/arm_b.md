## Answer

The first mistake is treating voice as a list of vocabulary words. Voice is a five-layer system: word choice, sentence syntax, rhythmic pacing, emotional register, and what the narrator notices versus ignores. Missing even one layer produces a book that sounds almost like the person but not quite — uncanny valley prose. Before writing a word of the memoir, you need to build a working model of all five.

The raw material is recordings, not drafts. Interview the subject extensively, on subjects that matter to them emotionally, and record everything. Then transcribe and mine the transcripts not for content but for pattern: How long are their sentences before they interrupt themselves? Do they use qualifiers compulsively ("I think," "maybe," "sort of") or do they assert flatly? What specific words do they return to? Where do they go quiet or shift register? What do they always omit when telling a story — those omissions are as much part of voice as what they include. Build a voice glossary of 20-30 characteristic expressions and structural habits from the transcripts. This becomes your reference artifact for the whole project.

The next threat is your own aesthetics leaking in under deadline pressure. This happens even to careful ghosts, especially at the syntactic level: the subject's words get stuffed into the ghost's sentence structures. The quality gate is reading drafts aloud, not silently. When a sentence sounds smooth and right to you but wrong when spoken, that's your rhythm, not theirs. Read every draft passage aloud before sending it, and have the subject read passages aloud to you during revision. If they stumble, that's the voice breaking.

One underrated element: trust. The subject's stiffness on the page is almost certainly anxiety-driven — they're performing writing rather than speaking. Your job before the structural work is to make them feel safe enough to be unguarded in conversation. The vivid, specific, idiosyncratic version of their voice only surfaces when they stop managing how they're perceived. The ghost who earns that trust gets a fundamentally different quality of raw material than the one who doesn't.

Finally, accept that you are not a tape recorder. You are building the best version of their voice — the voice they have when they're at their most themselves, distilled. This involves editorial judgment about which version of them is most true. That is a creative act, not just a transcription act. The goal is not to flatten yourself into them but to make yourself temporarily permeable to them, then write.
