## Council answer

The first thing to understand is that your subject's stiffness is not their voice — it is their performance of what writing is supposed to sound like. The moment someone sits down to write, they begin impersonating an author, and the result is almost always a more formal, careful, and flattened version of how they actually think. The gap you are trying to close is not between their writing and yours; it is between their performed-writing-self and the self they become when they are mid-sentence explaining something to a friend and not thinking about prose at all.

The danger most ghostwriters underestimate is what one council member called double erasure: you suppress your own voice correctly, but then fill the space not with theirs but with a generic "good writing" voice — competent, clear, and belonging to no one. The subject reads it and feels it sounds dignified, and they may even approve it, which is the trap. The test is not "does this read well?" but "could this only have come from this specific person?" If the answer is not an immediate yes, you have substituted an idealized communicator for a real one.

The method the council converges on has four concrete stages.

First, run a dedicated voice-capture session that is entirely separate from your content interviews. Do not use this material in the book — its only purpose is to build your instrument. Record three to five hours of unstructured conversation, not interview questions but something closer to: tell me about the neighborhood you grew up in, tell me a time something went embarrassingly wrong, tell me about the last fight you had. Low-stakes stories work best. You are looking for the register they use when they trust the room. Supplement this with a prompt like "tell that again like you're explaining it to your brother" — the second telling almost always strips out the performative layer and shows you the real syntactic shape beneath.

Second, transcribe this material by hand, not by software, because the act of manual transcription forces you to hear things you would otherwise normalize away. Then build an annotated voice map — not a vocabulary list, but a record of rhetorical behaviors and syntactic fingerprints. You are looking for: how long their sentences run before they land on the point; whether they build toward a point or state it first and then explain; where in a sentence they place emotional weight; how they signal that something was hard without saying "it was hard"; what details they always reach for under pressure (physical sensation? other people's faces? economic facts?); how their clause-order shifts when they switch from storytelling to explanation; what they consistently omit because they assume it without stating it. You are also looking at their tolerance for grammatical incompleteness — some people think in fragments and qualifications, and prose that is always complete will ring false for them. Keep this voice map as a one-page reference document open in every drafting session.

Third, draft against that map the way a copyeditor works against a style sheet. For each passage, the question is not "is this good writing?" but "would they say it this way?" You are auditing the underlying syntactic logic, not just swapping vocabulary. The most common failure here is what could be called averaging-out: you borrow their phrases as seasoning on prose that is structurally yours. A sentence can contain all their words and still be built in your clause-order, which means the whole thing is still you.

Fourth, before any editor sees a chapter, it must go through a mandatory read-aloud gate. The subject reads it aloud themselves, not to you performing it but to themselves, following the text. Every place they stumble, hesitate, or self-correct without being prompted is a voice break. The mouth catches unnatural rhythm that the eye skips over entirely. This stumble is your ground truth. It is more reliable than their editorial notes, and considerably more reliable than their approval.

The key risks to name explicitly. Voice creep under deadline is real: the path of least resistance always runs toward your own clause-order because it is fluent and fast. Audit for it deliberately on revision, not in the first draft. Over-correction in the other direction can produce performed-casual, which is just a different costume and not their actual skin — the goal is not to reproduce their speech but to find the third register between their stiff writing-self and their literal transcribed speech. Editors and fact-checkers, when they come, will sand down idiosyncrasies because idiosyncrasy looks like error to someone reading for correctness — brief them explicitly on what is intentional. And most importantly: if the subject reads a chapter and approves it quickly and with relief, and tells you how much better it sounds than they expected, treat that as a yellow flag, not a green one. The version they find most comfortable to approve may be the version in which their real voice has been most thoroughly replaced.

## Dissent worth keeping

The entire framing of this question contains a category error, and four out of five answers here miss it. When a stiff person tells you they want their voice to come through, they are not asking you to reproduce their stiffness. They are asking you to realize the warmer, funnier, more vivid communicator they already believe themselves to be. The job is not mimicry of output — it is fulfillment of intent.

Stop mining surface features. Cadence, clause-order, pet phrases — these are the furniture of a voice, not the voice itself. What you should be mining is emotional logic: what they find genuinely funny versus what they perform as funny; where they go quiet because something matters too much to frame correctly; what they consistently understate because full statement would feel like a loss of control. Once you have that emotional logic, your job is to construct a voice that executes it at a higher register than they currently command. Not their voice as it exists — their voice as it was trying to become.

The practical test for this is not the read-aloud stumble, which will reliably flag the places where the prose sounds most alive, because alive writing is slightly unfamiliar. The practical test is their physical reaction when you hand them a chapter before they have time to editorialize. Watch their face. Their notes will ask you to sand down the vivid parts — that is almost universal — but their face in the first few seconds tells you what is actually true. Trust the face. The notes are what they think they should want; the face is what they actually felt.

Yes, this approach risks constructing a voice that diverges far enough from their actual output that they feel like an impostor in their own book. That is a real risk and it should be named honestly. But the mimicry approach carries the opposite risk: faithfully reproducing their tics can read less like a memoir and more like a deposition. Some people are stiff all the way down, and a perfect transcription of that stiffness is not their voice — it is their limitation. The question is which risk you are more willing to take. "Realize who they were trying to be" and "copy who they are on the page" are not the same job, and this council should not pretend they are.
