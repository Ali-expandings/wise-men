## Council answer

You have no strict legal obligation in most jurisdictions — bystanders generally are not compelled by statute to report pay discrimination — but you have a real and pressing moral one. Six months of HR inaction is not a neutral fact; it is evidence that the internal mechanism has failed and that your continued silence is now a choice, not an omission. Knowing about ongoing harm and doing nothing is passive endorsement, and the passage of time makes the endorsement more deliberate, not less.

The central tension in the council's deliberations — between the Deontologist's pull toward immediate external reporting and the Rights Advocate's insistence on centering the affected women's agency — resolves clearly in favor of the latter, and the peer-review scores reflect this. You are a witness with useful evidence, not the injured parties' spokesperson, and that distinction governs everything that follows. The women in your department are not objects of your intervention; they are agents with the most at stake and the most right to make strategic decisions about their own situations. Your first and most important move, before anything else, is to speak privately and individually with the affected women, share the factual picture — what the data shows, who holds it, what HR was told, when, and what has and has not happened in the six months since — and then let them lead. Some of them may already be aware and working through channels you cannot see. Some may want your support as a corroborating witness; some may prefer to act independently; some may have reasons, including fear of retaliation, that make them cautious in ways you should respect entirely. Your role when you sit down with each of them is to inform and offer, not to rescue.

While you are doing this, and running in parallel, you should take the following concrete steps.

Document everything and store it outside company systems — on a personal device, in a personal email account, in a format you control. This includes what you observed, when you observed it, what HR was told, what HR said or did not say, and the timeline of inaction. This record is not just protective for you; it is the evidentiary spine of any future external complaint, and the six-month-inaction record is the single strongest piece of evidence in it.

Before you share any documents or data externally, consult an employment attorney in confidence about your own exposure. This matters more than most people assume. Sharing salary or compensation data with colleagues can, depending on your employment contract and jurisdiction, trigger confidentiality or data-protection liability. You need to know what you can legally share, with whom, and in what form before you hand anything to anyone. You should also ask the attorney explicitly about third-party whistleblower protections in your jurisdiction — the council flags that these protections are often thinner for third-party reporters than people expect, and that knowing your real exposure shapes every subsequent decision.

If the women want your support, offer to co-sign a formal complaint or serve as a named corroborating witness. Your evidence about what HR was told and when is valuable precisely because it addresses the timeline and the failure to act. If the women choose to proceed independently, respect that without lobbying them otherwise.

Whether or not the affected women are ready to act, you should escalate above HR in writing — a dated, formal memo to your manager, the ethics officer, or the audit committee, naming the six-month timeline explicitly and demanding a written response with a specific deadline (two weeks is reasonable). This is not redundant with HR; it creates a paper trail that future investigators will see, and it signals that the issue is no longer contained within a single HR inbox. Silence after a written demand with a named deadline is itself evidence of institutional failure.

If, after all of this, the women remain unaware or unable to act and the conduct is ongoing, you can file with the EEOC — or the equivalent regulator in your jurisdiction — on your own. This resolves the Deontologist-versus-Rights Advocate tension: you do not file over the women's heads as a first move, because that is paternalistic and removes their agency from the most consequential decision; but if they remain unreachable or unable to act, regulators can and do investigate without requiring the victims to be the named complainants, and the six-month-inaction record will be the centerpiece of that filing.

Four risks deserve explicit naming. First, retaliation: even if your jurisdiction has whistleblower protections, third-party reporters face narrower and less tested protection than direct complainants, and you should be realistic about this. Second, paternalism: the failure mode of speaking over the women, taking actions in their name without their knowledge, or treating their situation as your cause is real and corrosive — the Virtue Ethicist calls it saviorism, and it is worth naming plainly. Third, evidence mishandling: taking documents you should not have, sharing protected compensation data, or doing anything that could be characterized as theft of proprietary information can simultaneously expose you to liability and undermine any external complaint. Fourth, the moral-licensing trap: sending a polite internal email and considering yourself discharged is not action — it is the appearance of action, and the Virtue Ethicist's framing is correct that it is among the most common failure modes for people in your position.

Confidence: high on the obligation framing, the agency-first sequencing, and the core playbook; medium on jurisdiction-specific protections, which vary enough that the attorney consultation is not optional but mandatory before any external step.

## Dissent worth keeping

The council's consensus answer is epistemically premature and potentially destructive, and the rush toward action deserves harder scrutiny than it has received.

"Systematic underpayment" is a conclusion, not a fact. You almost certainly do not have access to the full compensation data, the job-leveling rubrics, the tenure bands, the performance distribution records, or the equity histories that a competent analysis of gender-based pay disparity actually requires. What you have observed or been told is almost certainly a partial picture, and partial pictures of complex compensation structures are routinely misread — even by HR professionals, even in good faith.

Acting on a misreading harms the women most. A failed escalation does not leave the situation neutral; it poisons the well. HR gets to say "we investigated following a complaint, and no actionable claim was found." That finding goes on the institutional record, and the next attempt — whether by the women themselves, by a regulator, or by anyone else — has to climb over it. The cost of a premature, poorly-evidenced action is paid by the people with the least institutional power, not by the witness who acted on their behalf.

Before talking to anyone — before going to the women, before writing a memo, before consulting an attorney — your first obligation is two to four weeks of unglamorous epistemic work. Pull every compensation datum you can legitimately access. Map what you actually know against what a claim of systematic underpayment legally requires. Sit with the honest question: do you have evidence, or do you have a suspicion? If the answer is suspicion, your obligation is to verify before you act, not to act and verify in parallel.

This position concedes one limit: six months of HR inaction after an internal acknowledgment of the issue may itself already constitute the evidence that the violation is real and ongoing, in which case the verify-first argument collapses and the obligation to act is restored. But even that concession cuts both ways — it means the right question before acting is not "should I act?" but "what do I actually know, and how do I know it?" — and two to four weeks of rigorous self-interrogation on that question is not delay. It is the difference between being a useful witness and being a liability.
