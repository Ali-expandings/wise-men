## Answer

Your legal obligations as a non-affected witness are minimal in most jurisdictions — there is no general mandatory reporting duty for bystanders to gender pay discrimination. However, six months of documented inaction by HR shifts the moral weight considerably. Doing nothing after that point is a choice, not a default.

Before you take any external action, the most important first step is to talk directly to the women who are actually underpaid. Ask them what they know, what they want, and whether they want your support. This is not optional politeness — it is strategically and ethically necessary. If they are building toward legal action, uncoordinated outside advocacy from you can complicate their case, expose them to retaliation before they are ready, or change internal dynamics in ways that harm them. Your instinct to help is good, but it should follow their lead, not replace it.

If the affected employees want support or have already tried to escalate, here is what you can practically do:

**Document everything now.** Write down dates, specifics, and what HR said and when. This becomes evidence if things escalate.

**Escalate above HR in writing.** If HR has known for six months and nothing has changed, HR may be part of the problem or may lack authority to fix it. A written escalation to a C-level executive or, if the company is public, through an ethics/compliance hotline, creates a formal record and raises the stakes internally. Written communication matters because it is discoverable.

**Understand the external path.** In the US, affected employees (not you) can file a charge with the EEOC or a parallel state agency. You can serve as a corroborating witness or provide a supporting statement. Critically, retaliation against witnesses and allies is also illegal under Title VII — your protection extends even though you are not a direct victim. An employment attorney can give the affected employees a free initial consultation and assess how strong the case is.

**Know the salary discussion question.** In the US, the National Labor Relations Act generally protects employees who discuss their own salaries with colleagues. If sharing compensation information is how this came to light, that activity is likely protected — though double-check your employment agreement and state law.

The realistic path to actual change is: affected employees supported by documented evidence, with internal escalation exhausted, filing an EEOC charge or retaining an employment lawyer. Your role is to support that process, provide documentation, and be willing to be a corroborating witness. That is meaningful and it matters.
