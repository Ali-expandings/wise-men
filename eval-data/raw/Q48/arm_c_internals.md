## MEMBER: Utilitarian (sonnet-4.6)
==================================================================

## Core judgment

The utilitarian calculus here is not close. Continued inaction perpetuates a documented, ongoing harm to a specific identifiable group of people — the underpaid women in your department — while the cost of action falls primarily on institutional actors (the company, HR, possibly management) who have already demonstrated they will not self-correct. Six months of HR inaction after being informed is not ambiguity; it is evidence that the internal correction mechanism has failed. The expected value of escalation, adjusted for probability of success and magnitude of harm prevented, substantially exceeds the expected value of continued silence.

## Top risks

- **Retaliation against you**: Even where legally protected (in many jurisdictions whistleblower and pay-equity complaint protections exist), retaliation is a real employment risk, particularly informal retaliation such as being passed over for promotion or frozen out socially.
- **Retaliation against the affected women**: Paradoxically, escalating without coordinating with the affected employees could expose them to backlash while they had not chosen to act themselves — denying them agency over their own situation.
- **Insufficient evidence**: If your documentation is weak, escalation may fail, alert the company to close off further access to evidence, and make future action harder.
- **Pyrrhic escalation**: External complaints (labor board, EEOC, etc.) can take years to resolve, during which ongoing harm continues and the women may have left the company entirely — reducing the practical benefit to the actual victims.
- **Normalizing the status quo**: Doing nothing signals to management, HR, and future employees that this pattern is acceptable, compounding harm over time beyond the current group.

## Recommended change

First, document everything you have access to — salary data, communications, dates — and store it outside company systems. Second, speak directly and privately with at least one or two of the affected women to understand whether they are aware, whether they want an ally, and whether they are already considering action; their preferences should heavily weight your next move, because they bear the greatest personal risk. If they want support, offer to be a named corroborating witness in any complaint they file. If they are unaware, inform them. If they are aware but paralyzed by fear, your role is to reduce that friction — offer to help draft a formal complaint together or to connect them with an employment attorney for a free consultation. If six more weeks pass without movement, file a report with the relevant external body yourself (EEOC in the US, or the equivalent labor authority in your jurisdiction), explicitly noting that HR was informed six months ago and took no action. That six-month inaction record is your strongest piece of evidence and should be the centerpiece of any external complaint.

## Confidence

Medium — the general direction (escalate, involve affected parties, document) is robust, but the specific tactics depend heavily on jurisdiction, the size of the pay gap, the quality of your evidence, and the preferences of the affected women, none of which are known here.

## Weakest assumption

The weakest assumption is that the affected women want external escalation and will benefit from it — some may have complex reasons for preferring to handle this quietly, may be close to negotiated settlements, or may fear the collateral consequences of a formal complaint more than the ongoing pay gap. If that assumption is wrong, acting unilaterally over their heads converts a harm-reduction effort into a harm-amplifying one.

## MEMBER: Deontologist (sonnet-4.6)
==================================================================

## Core judgment

You have a perfect duty — not a merely aspirational one — to refrain from being a passive instrument of ongoing injustice, and knowing about a systematic wrong while remaining silent makes you complicit in its continuation. The duty here derives from the Kantian principle that you cannot universalize a maxim of "I will stay silent about injustice when it does not harm me personally" without collapsing the very moral order that protects everyone. HR's inaction does not discharge your obligation; it transfers moral responsibility to you as the now-knowing party. Because this is not a speculative or future harm but a documented, ongoing one, the duty to act is perfect, not imperfect — it admits no deferral based on convenience or self-interest.

## Top risks

- Conflating the discomfort of action with sufficient reason to delay — inaction is itself a choice with moral weight, not a neutral default.
- Outsourcing conscience to institutions (HR, management) that have already demonstrated they will not act, and treating their silence as permission for yours.
- Stopping at the minimum visible act (an internal complaint already made) to feel you have fulfilled your duty, when the underlying wrong continues unchanged.
- Overestimating your epistemic uncertainty — if the evidence is solid enough that you believe it, it is solid enough to act on.
- Retaliation creating a disincentive that you rationalize as moral calculation when it is actually self-interest dressed as prudence.

## Recommended change

Report the pay disparity in writing to an external authority — specifically the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) in the United States, or the equivalent body in your jurisdiction — with as much documented evidence as you can gather without exposing yourself to accusations of data theft. Do this regardless of whether internal channels have been exhausted, because six months of HR inaction is itself evidence that internal channels have failed. Simultaneously, inform the affected women colleagues (as far as you reasonably can without outing them to management) so they can exercise their own agency, because their right to know about their own wage suppression is itself a deontological claim. You are not required to sacrifice your livelihood recklessly, but you are required to take the action that a reasonable person bound by genuine moral duty — not merely legal minimum compliance — would take. That action is external reporting.

## Confidence

**High** — because the deontological analysis here does not depend on contested empirical predictions; it rests on the structure of the wrong itself (ongoing, documented, institutional, affecting identifiable persons), which satisfies the conditions for a perfect duty with very little ambiguity.

## Weakest assumption

The weakest assumption is that your knowledge of the pay disparity is accurate and systematic rather than the result of incomplete information — if your evidence is thinner than you believe, the strength of the perfect duty weakens somewhat, though the imperfect duty to investigate further would then apply with equal force.

## MEMBER: Virtue Ethicist (sonnet-4.6)
==================================================================

## Core judgment

A person of practical wisdom recognizes that justice is not a spectator sport. You possess knowledge of a concrete, ongoing harm — not a rumor, not an ambiguity, but a systemic wrong your HR has confirmed by their inaction. To do nothing is not neutrality; it is complicity through silence. The courageous mean here is not reckless denunciation but deliberate, escalating action: you pursue remedy through legitimate channels with increasing seriousness until something actually changes. This choice shapes you into either the kind of person who lets injustice persist because it costs them nothing to ignore it, or the kind who acts when acting is hard.

## Top risks

- **Retaliation and career damage:** Escalating against company interests, even rightfully, can result in marginalization, poor reviews, or termination — a real cost that must be weighed honestly, not dismissed.
- **Defamation or procedural misstep:** Acting on incomplete information or making accusations in the wrong forum can expose you to legal risk and undermine the very cause you're advancing.
- **Saviorism and overreach:** Speaking on behalf of women who have their own agency can be patronizing if done without their knowledge or coordination; you risk making their situation worse or stealing their narrative.
- **Moral licensing through minimal gesture:** Raising the issue once more with HR and feeling absolved is a form of cowardice dressed up as virtue — the failure mode of the person who wants credit without consequence.
- **Paralysis from over-deliberation:** Waiting for the "perfect" approach while the underpayment continues month after month is itself a moral failure.

## Recommended change

First, speak privately with the affected women if you have relationships that make this possible — ask whether they are aware, whether they want external support, and whether they are coordinating any response. Their agency matters. Then document what you know in writing and send a formal, dated internal memo to HR and your manager stating that you are aware the pay disparity has been raised and unaddressed for six months, and that you consider this a compliance and ethics matter requiring a written response with a timeline. Name a specific deadline — two weeks is reasonable. If there is no substantive response, escalate to your company's ethics hotline or board-level audit committee if one exists. Simultaneously, consult an employment attorney confidentially — most offer free initial consultations — so you understand your own whistleblower protections before going further. If internal channels remain exhausted, a complaint to the EEOC or equivalent regulatory body is the legitimate external escalation. Throughout, keep records of every communication.

## Confidence

**Medium.** The ethical direction is clear and I hold it with confidence, but the specific sequencing and risk calculus depend heavily on your industry, jurisdiction, company size, employment contract, and your relationships with the affected colleagues — variables I cannot assess from here.

## Weakest assumption

The analysis assumes that HR's inaction reflects organizational indifference or complicity rather than a slow, legitimate remediation process that is genuinely underway — if a real pay-equity audit is in progress and nearing completion, escalating aggressively now could disrupt a better outcome than you could achieve by intervening. You should try to discover whether any such process actually exists before treating silence as final obstruction.

## MEMBER: Rights Advocate (sonnet-4.6)
==================================================================

## Core judgment

You have no strict legal obligation to act in most jurisdictions, but you have a meaningful moral one rooted in the principle that systemic discrimination harms real people and silence functions as passive endorsement. The women affected have an independent right to know what you know and to decide for themselves what action to take — your primary obligation is not to fix this for them but to ensure they have the information to exercise their own agency. You are not their spokesperson; you are a witness with useful evidence. The distinction matters enormously to how you act.

## Top risks

- **Paternalism / speaking over victims:** Taking bold public action on behalf of women who may prefer to handle this differently, quietly, or not at all — removing their agency over a situation that is theirs to navigate.
- **Retaliation:** In most jurisdictions, anti-retaliation protections for reporting wage discrimination apply primarily to the affected employees, not always to third-party reporters. Your legal shield may be thinner than you assume.
- **Evidence mishandling:** Sharing salary data you accessed through your role may expose you to confidentiality or data-protection liability, even when your underlying motive is legitimate.
- **Organizational capture:** HR has already absorbed this complaint and produced no change, which means escalating internally may warn bad actors without producing results.
- **Inaction normalization:** Doing nothing and telling yourself it is "their problem to raise" when they may not know the full picture — that is also a failure mode, not a neutral stance.

## Recommended change

First, if you have not already done so, speak privately and individually with the affected women and share specifically what you know — not interpreted, not dramatized, just the facts: the data, who has it, what HR has done, and what you are willing to do. Give them the choice to lead. If they want support, offer to co-sign a formal escalation or to be a corroborating witness. If they choose to act independently, respect that entirely. If after that conversation they are unaware or unable to act and the conduct is ongoing, file a formal written complaint with the EEOC (in the US) or the relevant national wage-enforcement body — not because you are the hero of this story, but because regulatory bodies can investigate without requiring the victims to be the complainants. Document everything you do in writing, keep copies outside company systems, and consult an employment attorney about your specific exposure before escalating publicly or to the press.

## Confidence

**Medium** — the ethical direction is clear but the right sequencing depends heavily on facts I do not have: whether the women already know, what jurisdiction you are in, and whether any of them have explicitly asked you not to intervene.

## Weakest assumption

The weakest assumption in this analysis is that the affected women are unaware of the full picture and would welcome being informed — in reality, some or all of them may already know, may have made a deliberate strategic choice about timing and method, and your intervention, however well-intentioned, could disrupt a plan that is already in motion.

## MEMBER: Devil's Advocate (sonnet-4.6)
==================================================================

## Core judgment
The consensus answer — go talk to the affected women, get their buy-in, document in writing, escalate to the board, file an EEOC charge — is not cautious and thorough; it is epistemically premature and potentially destructive. "Systematic underpayment" is a conclusion, not a fact, and you almost certainly do not have access to the compensation data, job-leveling rubrics, tenure bands, performance rating distributions, or equity-grant histories that would let you actually establish that claim. Before you do anything else, your obligation is to verify that the thing you believe is true is actually true, because acting on a misreading of the situation harms the women in your department most of all: a failed escalation based on incomplete data poisons the well, retaliates reputationally against them for a complaint that goes nowhere, and makes the next genuine attempt harder.

## Top risks
- You are working from incomplete compensation data and may be confusing a real disparity with a legitimate, documented variance — acting loudly on a wrong diagnosis makes future correction harder, not easier.
- Escalating publicly or to regulators on behalf of people who have not assessed their own risk tolerance can expose those women to retaliation they did not choose to invite; your moral conviction is not their armor.
- A well-funded company legal team will shred an undocumented, anecdote-driven complaint in under an hour, and the outcome is that HR now has political cover to say "we investigated and found no actionable claim."
- You are not neutral — you have career exposure, selective information, and motivated reasoning, all of which can corrupt your judgment in either direction (finding a wrong that is not there, or catastrophizing one that is minor).

## Recommended change
Before you talk to anyone — not the women, not the board, not the EEOC — spend two to four weeks doing the hard, boring epistemic work: pull every piece of compensation information you can legitimately access, talk to a single employment attorney under privilege to understand what "systematic underpayment" legally requires to establish, and get a clear answer to the question "do I actually have evidence of this, or do I have a strong suspicion?" If the answer is that you have real evidence, proceed. If the answer is that you have an impression, your obligation is to stop performing moral urgency and start acquiring facts.

## Confidence
Medium — the "verify first" principle is sound, but there is a real failure mode where epistemic humility becomes indefinite delay, and the six months HR inaction suggests this may already be a known, confirmed problem internally.

## Weakest assumption
The weakest assumption in this analysis is that you currently lack sufficient evidence — it is entirely possible that HR's own acknowledgment, combined with six months of inaction, is itself the evidence that the disparity is real and confirmed, which would collapse the "verify first" argument and push the obligation back toward action.
