If the research genuinely requires the PhD credential to do the work you actually want to do — not to signal competence, not to have a title, but because the lab access, the collaborators, the structured deep time, and the institutional infrastructure are prerequisites for the specific thing you're trying to learn or build — then it can be worth it. That's a real version. Some research problems are genuinely inaccessible outside academia, and some people hit a ceiling in industry work that only doctoral-level immersion breaks through.

But most people who are "genuinely curious about the research" are curious in a way that doesn't actually require five to seven years of structured misery, credential politics, and income sacrifice to satisfy. You can read deeply, collaborate with labs informally, write, build, attend conferences, even audit courses. The question worth sitting with is: have you actually hit the wall of what curiosity without a PhD allows you to do, or are you imagining the PhD will feel like sustained intellectual freedom? Because it often doesn't. It's a job with a difficult manager (your advisor), uncertain timelines, and a lot of unglamorous work that isn't the interesting part you're picturing.

The "no academic career" part is worth taking seriously too. The PhD is optimized for producing academics. If you want to do research in industry, government, think tanks, or independent work, the credential helps in some fields and is largely irrelevant in others. Which field matters enormously here — biomedical, computer science, economics, and social sciences all have very different post-PhD landscapes outside academia.

The romanticization question: probably yes, some of it is. The version of PhD life people imagine — reading, thinking, long conversations, intellectual community — exists, but it's maybe 20% of the experience. The rest is administrative, political, uncertain, and isolating in a specific way that surprises almost everyone who comes from high-functioning industry roles.

The honest test is this: can you name the specific research question or problem you want to work on? Not the field, not the vibe — the actual question. If you can, then figure out where that question is being worked on and talk to the people doing it. If they tell you you need the credential to contribute meaningfully, and if the work itself still matters enough after you've seen the inside of it, then go.
