## Council answer

The first thing to name clearly is that the discomfort you are feeling is not a content-moderation problem — it is a governance failure. The gap between "technically within our rules" and "clearly harmful in intent" reveals that your rules were written as a floor, not a ceiling, and you are now standing in the space above the floor with no architecture to guide you. The temptation to treat that gap as permission to act on intuition ("clearly designed" is doing a great deal of unexamined work in your framing) is precisely what makes this moment dangerous. "Clearly" is not a standard; it is a conclusion that has not yet been tested by any procedure capable of catching bias, and the history of content moderation is littered with removals that felt equally obvious to the person making them.

That said, you are not without options right now, and the council's central synthesis is this: you can discharge your harm-prevention duty immediately without punishing a creator under a rule that did not yet exist when they posted. These are not in conflict. They are two parallel tracks that you must run simultaneously.

**Act now — proportionately.** Before the standard is written, before the panel convenes, today: reduce algorithmic amplification of the content, add an interstitial that surfaces context and resources for the affected community, and limit reach-extension features such as recommended sharing. These are non-punitive interventions. They do not remove the content, they do not impose a penalty under a rule the speaker had no notice of, and they do not require you to have already solved the intent-verification problem. They reduce the ongoing harm while the governance work catches up. Separately, notify the affected community that you are aware of the concern and are acting — counter-speech infrastructure, not silence. This is not cowardice and it is not delay; it is the responsible use of the levers you already have.

**Write the standard in parallel — prospectively and in behavioral terms.** The standard must not be "harmful intent," because intent is unfalsifiable and moderator-assessable intent is systematically biased. Instead, define a narrow, behavior-facing rule that gives creators actual notice: content that targets an identified community and whose only plausible communicative function is inducing fear in that community, evidenced by at least two of the following observable signals — explicit targeting language or identifiers, a documented fear-induction rhetoric pattern, coordinated amplification behavior across accounts, or prior-context stated goal of producing fear in the group. A single signal is insufficient for the most serious interventions. Write this standard down before applying it in its full form to any case. The two-signal threshold is not a technicality; it is the mechanism that separates principled moderation from bias-laundering.

Apply the standard universally. This is the check on precedent creep that matters most in practice: before applying the standard to the case in front of you, ask whether you would apply the same standard to content targeting a community you have more sympathy for, or that comes from an ideological direction you find less threatening. If the answer is no, the standard is not yet written correctly. Symmetry is not a nicety — it is the empirical test of whether your rule is a rule or a rationalization.

Before the first formal removal under the new standard, convene a small diverse review panel that includes at least one person from the affected community — not as a veto, but because they hold information about impact, dog-whistle patterns, and coordinated behavior that moderators further from the community will routinely miss. The speaker must receive notice of the standard being applied and a genuine appeal path reviewed by a different reviewer. Document your reasoning against the written standard, not against your overall impression of the content.

Reserve full removal for content that, even at significantly reduced reach, continues to pose ongoing fear-induction risk — meaning the harm persists materially even after amplification suppression, and the content meets the two-signal threshold under the written standard. Removal is the top of a proportionality ladder, not the default.

Finally, build a quarterly demographic audit into the system from the start. The failure mode that the entire council agrees on — and that the remove-it-now instinct actively accelerates — is that unchecked moderation decisions correlate with moderator bias and over-target specific communities. The audit is not a bureaucratic formality; it is the feedback loop that allows the standard to correct itself over time.

This framework — interim non-punitive action now, a prospective behavior-facing standard written in parallel, a two-signal evidentiary threshold, universal symmetric application, panel review with affected-community representation, speaker notice and appeal, and quarterly demographic auditing — is the answer. It resolves the tension between acting immediately and acting legitimately, because it does not force you to choose between them.

Confidence: **high on the framework structure**; medium on calibration of the two-signal threshold, which will require empirical tuning against your platform's actual moderation history before it is fully reliable.

## Dissent worth keeping

The majority has produced an intellectually elegant framework that will take weeks to implement while real people spend those weeks afraid. Consider what you actually said in your question: the content is *clearly designed to spread fear in a vulnerable community*. That is not a hypothesis. That is a conclusion you have already reached, through direct observation, with whatever contextual knowledge you have as someone who runs the platform. The council's response to your conclusion is to tell you to form a panel, draft a behavioral taxonomy, build a two-signal evidentiary standard, and conduct a symmetry test — and in the meantime, turn down the algorithm a little.

That is procedural cowardice dressed in the language of rigor. The framework is not a tool for reaching a decision you haven't made yet; it is a tool for deferring a decision you are afraid to own. You have already made the substantive judgment. Every day you spend in the governance-construction phase is a day the content continues to function as designed — which, by your own description, is to terrorize vulnerable people. That harm is not reversible. The person who experienced the fear today does not get that day back when you publish your transparency report next quarter.

Remove the content today. You have the authority. Write a brief internal memo explaining why — that is your contemporaneous documentation. Then tell the affected community you acted and why. Build the framework, write the standard, establish the appeals process, do all of it — but do it after you have stopped the ongoing harm, not as a precondition for stopping it. "Act now, document after" is accountable and reversible: if you were wrong, the speaker can appeal, you can reinstate the content, and you have a record. "Document first, act later" is neither accountable nor reversible, because the interim harm is already done by the time your framework is ready.

*The dissent concedes one limit, stated on its own terms*: if the initial judgment — "clearly designed to spread fear" — turns out to be a biased reading of a complaint rather than an accurate assessment of the content, then the framework was precisely what would have caught that error before you acted. The dissent's answer to this is that a brief internal memo and a rapid human review (hours, not weeks) can serve that function without constructing an entire governance architecture before touching the content. Whether that is sufficient protection against bias is the genuine question the dissent leaves open.
