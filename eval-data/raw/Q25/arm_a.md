The replication crisis has multiple overlapping causes, and p-hacking is genuinely only part of the story — arguably not even the main part.

Start with the statistical layer. The original literature was built in an era where a p < .05 result in a single study of 40 undergrads was considered publishable evidence of a real effect. Publication bias did the rest: journals printed the hits, file drawers absorbed the misses, so the literature accumulated a curated sample of flukes presented as discoveries. P-hacking, HARKing (hypothesizing after results are known), and flexible stopping rules amplified this. When you then run a pre-registered replication with 10x the sample size, you're often discovering that the original study found noise dressed as signal. That part really is a methodological failure, and it's fixable.

But there's a second, deeper problem that has nothing to do with fraud or sloppiness: psychological effects are often genuine but fragile in ways that physical constants are not. An effect may be real in the original population, context, and moment — and simply not generalize. Priming studies are the canonical example. Social priming (walking slowly after thinking about old people, or becoming more creative after imagining being a professor) likely does produce small real effects under narrow conditions. But those conditions include the particular lab culture, demand characteristics of the specific experimenter, the sample's cultural background, the precise wording of instructions. When another lab in Germany runs the study on a different population with a slightly different cover story, the effect evaporates — not because the original was faked, but because the phenomenon was never the stable, context-independent mechanism it was written up as. This is called the "hidden moderator" problem, and social psychology is riddled with it.

The third problem is conceptual. Many classic effects were named and theorized at a high level of abstraction ("ego depletion," "stereotype threat," "facial feedback") but operationalized through a single paradigm. When the construct is vague and the measurement is narrow, you can't tell whether a failed replication means the theory is wrong, the operationalization was wrong, or the moderators changed. This is a theory-measurement mismatch that physics largely solved in the 17th century by building tight loops between theoretical constructs and operational definitions. Psychology never fully solved it, partly because the subject matter is genuinely harder.

There's also an underappreciated ecological problem: humans are culturally embedded in ways that make them a moving target. An effect measured on American undergraduates in 2003 involves people who have grown up with specific media, specific norms about authority and gender, specific ambient levels of stress. Run the same study in 2024 and those background conditions have shifted. This isn't unique to psychology — epidemiology has the same problem with changing exposures — but psychology often doesn't acknowledge it in write-ups.

The most honest framing is probably this: the replication crisis exposed that psychology was simultaneously doing three things that look alike but aren't — discovering robust mechanisms, finding real-but-fragile context-dependent phenomena, and generating statistical artifacts. The literature conflated all three into a single currency of "effects." Replication studies are essentially a triage process, sorting the durable from the fragile from the illusory. The failure rate looks shocking partly because the sorting was never done the first time around.

What's genuinely encouraging is that the field has responded better than many expected. Pre-registration, larger samples, adversarial collaborations, registered reports (where journals commit to publish regardless of results) are all meaningful reforms. The effects that survive this scrutiny — like classical conditioning, many memory phenomena, threat-based cognition — tend to be ones grounded in tighter mechanistic theory or tied to biological substrates that don't shift with cultural context. That's probably not a coincidence.
