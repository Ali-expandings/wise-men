## Answer

Take the Senior IC offer. The "should be more strategic" feeling is the wrong framing doing most of the heavy lifting here — it conflates "strategic" with "management," which is a cultural script, not a career truth. Staff/Principal ICs at smaller companies make architecture decisions, set technical direction, influence roadmaps, and have outsized leverage. That is strategy.

Three concrete reasons this leans IC:

**Reversibility asymmetry.** IC → manager is easy and common at any age. Manager → IC reads as a demotion culturally and is genuinely hard to execute. If you decide you want to manage at 36 with a strong senior IC track record, you'll be a *better* manager — technical credibility intact, managing from genuine authority. The reverse path is much messier.

**The "should" signal.** You said "I like coding more than meetings" plainly and honestly. You said "I feel like I should" — not "I want to." That word "should" almost always points at external pressure (peers, LinkedIn, social scripts about what 32 is supposed to look like). If no one could see your title, what would you pick? You already answered it.

**Base rates on motivation.** Engineers who go manager because they felt they should — rather than because they genuinely want to grow people — tend to be mediocre managers and unhappy ones. The cost isn't just to you; it's to your future reports.

The smaller company risk (it might fail) is real but manageable: strong IC skills at a well-scoped senior level are the most portable thing in tech. A company failing 18 months in is a story, not a scar.

## Dissent worth keeping

The hybrid EM window is real and the devil's advocate version of it is worth sitting with: at 32 with deep technical credibility, you're in a narrow window where you can enter management while still being technically relatable and respected by ICs. At 40, you're competing against people who have 8 years of management reps. If you ever *do* want the EM/tech-lead archetype — which carries genuine career power — starting now compresses the learning curve while your technical floor is still high. This isn't a reason to override your authentic preference, but if there's any ambiguity in that preference you haven't admitted to yourself, this window matters.
