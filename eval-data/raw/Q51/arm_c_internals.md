## Core judgment
The senior IC offer is likely the better move, and the "I should be more strategic" framing is the red flag here — not the decision itself. Reversibility is asymmetric. Going IC → management is moderately reversible (you can try management, hate it, and return to IC with a story). Going management → IC is harder: your technical skills decay, your identity shifts, and the market re-prices you as a "lapsed engineer turned manager" rather than a senior IC. The smaller company IC offer is the more optionality-preserving path because it keeps your technical compound interest running while you gather more data on whether management actually appeals to you.

Base rates are not friendly to reluctant managers. People who take management roles because they feel they "should" — not because they genuinely want to develop others and own org problems — wash out or quietly suffer at a high rate. The ones who thrive in management are usually pulled toward it, not pushed by career-clock anxiety at 32.

## Top risks
- You take the IC role, enjoy it, but stall because the smaller company has a shallower senior-to-staff ladder and you hit a ceiling faster than expected.
- You take the management role, discover you are good at it but miserable, and have burned 2-3 years of technical compounding in the process.
- The "feel like I should" pressure does not go away at senior IC — it resurfaces at staff/principal and you make the same reluctant jump later under worse conditions.
- The smaller company's size is a proxy risk you have not evaluated: runway, growth trajectory, and quality of the technical org matter more than headcount.
- You conflate "strategic career" with "upward org chart movement" — senior IC at a high-growth smaller company can be far more strategically valuable than middle management at a stagnant larger one.

## Recommended change
Before deciding, spend 2 hours mapping what a "good outcome at 35" actually looks like in concrete terms (compensation target, type of work, autonomy level, optionality for what comes after) — this collapses the "should" pressure into a real constraint you can evaluate both offers against.

## Confidence
Medium — the reversibility and base-rate logic is solid, but the answer is sensitive to facts you have not shared (smaller company's trajectory, what the management role actually involves, your financial situation).

## Weakest assumption
The core argument assumes the smaller company's senior IC role has a viable growth path to staff/principal — if the org is too flat or too small to support that progression, the optionality calculus flips.

## Core judgment
Take the manager track. "I like coding more than meetings" is not a preference — it is a skill gap you are rationalizing as a personality trait. At 32, you are at the exact inflection point where the engineers who compound fastest are the ones who learn leverage, not the ones who double down on what already feels comfortable. The senior IC role at a smaller company is not a promotion — it is a lateral move dressed up as one, and the comfort of "more coding" is precisely what makes it dangerous. You are choosing a known ceiling over an uncomfortable growth curve and calling it self-knowledge.

The manager track at your current company is not a trap — it is a forcing function. You will be bad at it at first. That badness is the point. The skills you build managing people — reading organizational dynamics, influencing without authority, making decisions under ambiguity with incomplete information — are the exact skills that separate staff and principal ICs from senior ICs who plateau and wonder why they stopped getting promoted at 35. The engineers who become genuinely elite individual contributors almost universally understand management deeply, even if they eventually return to IC. You cannot skip the curriculum.

The smaller company framing is doing a lot of work here that you should interrogate. "Slightly smaller" almost always means less internal mobility, thinner comp bands, higher acquisition/shutdown risk, and a shallower network. The senior IC title is more prestigious-sounding at a place where there are fewer people to compare yourself against. That is not a career move — that is a comfort move with a better job title.

## Top risks
- IC ceilings are real and close: senior IC at a smaller company is often a terminal level, with staff/principal requiring headcount that small companies don't have
- "I like coding more than meetings" at 32 is avoidance behavior, not identity — the meetings you hate are a skill you haven't built, not a personality incompatibility
- Smaller company comp bands compress faster; the delta between your current comp and the new offer likely narrows or reverses within 3 years as your current company promotes you
- Leaving your current company means losing accumulated political capital, internal reputation, and sponsor relationships that are the actual currency of career advancement
- The senior IC role optimizes for your next 2 years; the manager track optimizes for your next 20

## Recommended change
Stay at your current company, take the manager track, and treat the discomfort of meetings as the exact signal that this is the skill you need to build — not evidence you've chosen wrong.

## Confidence
Medium — the manager track argument is strong, but it depends entirely on whether your current company's management path is actually functional and not a political graveyard.

## Weakest assumption
The argument breaks entirely if your current company's management culture is toxic or if the manager role being offered has no real authority — a manager track at a dysfunctional org is worse than any IC role anywhere.
