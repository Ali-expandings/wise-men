Genuinely hard situation, and the fact that it's bothering you suggests you care about doing it right — that actually matters here.

The core tension: they haven't told you because they don't feel safe to, or because it's just not your business yet. Either way, acting on information they didn't share puts you in an awkward position relationally.

My honest take: don't bring it up directly. "I've noticed you seem to be looking around" almost always backfires — it feels like surveillance, kills trust, and puts them on the defensive even if you mean it kindly. The conversation they'd need to have with you is now tainted by the fact that you were watching.

But silent planning has its own failure mode. If you quietly start backfilling or deprioritizing their projects without them knowing why, they feel it. People are attuned to being managed out or overlooked, and it can actually accelerate their exit or damage the relationship permanently.

The better frame: treat this like a signal, not a fact. Job searching doesn't always mean leaving. People search when they feel undervalued, unchallenged, underpaid, or just curious. The most useful response to the signal is to address the underlying conditions, not the symptom.

So practically — have a genuine conversation about their trajectory, career development, and what they want. Not "I know you're looking," just "I want to make sure I'm setting you up for what you actually want, what would that look like?" You create an opening without making them confess or feel cornered.

At the same time, do your contingency planning — not as punishment, but as good management. You should always have some sense of what happens if any of your key reports left. That's just responsibility.

If they eventually tell you, receive it graciously. The goal is to be the kind of manager where leaving is a conversation, not a surprise resignation. That's built before the moment, not during it.
