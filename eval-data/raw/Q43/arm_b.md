## Answer

Do both things at once: plan quietly for continuity, and actively open a door in your next 1:1.

The first part is just competent management — document their work, identify knowledge gaps, start low-key cross-training. Do this regardless of the job search suspicion; you should be doing it anyway.

The second part is where people get passive when they shouldn't. You don't confront the job search directly — you have no confirmed information and cornering someone on inference is humiliating even when correct. But you do create a real opening. Something like: "I want to check in — is this role giving you what you need right now? If something isn't working, I'd rather know so we can try to fix it." That's not a trap. It's an invitation. If they're leaving because of something fixable — comp, growth path, a bad dynamic — you might keep them. If they're leaving for reasons you can't fix, you've signaled you're safe to be honest with, and the handoff will be cleaner.

The option of "act like you don't know and do nothing" is the worst of the three. It leaves you operationally exposed and closes the relational door. The discomfort you're feeling is the signal — silence here is avoidance dressed as respect.

## Dissent worth keeping

The careful "create an opening" framing is still managing around the truth. If you actually have a real relationship with this person, the most honest move is more direct: "I want to be the kind of manager you can be honest with about your career. If you're thinking about what's next, I'd rather know — I'll advocate for you either way." That's vulnerable. It also treats them like an adult instead of a chess piece to position around. The subtle approach, however tactful, can still feel like cowardice when the person eventually looks back.

## Confidence

Medium — the "open a door" answer is widely applicable, but the right call depends heavily on the actual relationship, what you think they'd receive well, and what specifically they might be leaving for.
