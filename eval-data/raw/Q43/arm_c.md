## Council answer

Four of five members converge clearly: do not confront the job search on private inference. The convergence holds across utilitarian, deontological, rights-based, and virtue-ethical lines, which is a signal worth trusting. The shared logic: you hold intelligence the other person doesn't know you have, acting on it creates an unacknowledged power asymmetry, and confronting them with it — however gently framed — weaponizes information they never consented to you using in a decision role. Job searching is legitimate self-determination, not a breach of duty to you.

But do nothing is the worst option. Silent planning while feigning ignorance is double-dealing — acting on information the other person doesn't know you have. The virtuous critique is precise: you can't un-know what you know, so the question isn't "act or don't act" but "how do you act cleanly."

The move — which you should be executing regardless of whether you had this signal — is a genuine, uncoded career conversation. Not a coded fishing expedition. Not "how are things going" as a euphemism. A real one: what's engaging you right now, where you want to grow in the next year, what you would change if you could change one thing about the role. This addresses the conditions that might be driving the search without requiring disclosure you aren't owed. If they volunteer something, you plan openly together. If they don't, you gave a real opening and can proceed with contingency planning from a clean conscience.

Quiet continuity planning — cross-training, documentation, succession for any key report — is standard good management and carries no ethical weight as long as it doesn't translate into deprioritizing this person's projects, freezing their growth, or building a replacement that disadvantages them before they've left. That line matters.

Practical order of operations: (1) Have the genuine career conversation within the week, with all your direct reports if possible so it reads as normal practice. (2) Run contingency planning in your own planning documents as you should for any key role. (3) When and if they disclose, shift to open collaborative transition planning. (4) If they never disclose and never leave, you ran good management.

## Dissent worth keeping

The Devil's Advocate dissents at full strength and the position deserves to be stated directly, not softened.

Confront directly and early. The "quiet career conversation" approach fails the most important stakeholder: you. Silent planning treats the problem as static; if this person is good enough to worry about losing, they're good enough for an honest adult conversation.

The concrete failure mode is the Cascade: you plan quietly, they see work redistributed, projects frozen, growth deprioritized, they read it — correctly — as being written off, the search accelerates from "exploring" to "urgent," they leave in 6 weeks instead of 6 months, you lose transition time because the silent planning telegraphed itself, other team members notice, anxiety spreads, and you now have a retention problem you didn't have before. The silent approach assumes the other person is passive. They are not.

The recommended direct opening: "I want to have a candid conversation. I've noticed some signals you might be thinking about what's next. I'm not trying to corner you — I want to understand where your head is so I can be useful to you and plan well. You don't owe me anything here, but I'd rather talk openly." This reframes accusation as partnership and gives a face-saving exit if you're wrong.

Conceded weakness: this assumes enough relational trust that "direct" doesn't read as threatening or retaliatory. If the reason they're searching is you specifically, directness can accelerate the departure or trigger an HR complaint.
