This is a genuinely important tension, and you're not misreading anything — you've stumbled onto one of the most common and dangerous illusions in SaaS metrics. The short answer is that NPS and retention measure completely different things at completely different moments, and high NPS can absolutely coexist with high churn. Here's why.

NPS captures a sentiment snapshot. When someone fills out an NPS survey, they're answering "how do you feel about this product right now, in this moment?" That moment is typically after a positive interaction — after onboarding when the novelty is high, after a support ticket got resolved well, after they just achieved something with the product. Promoters are telling you the truth: they genuinely liked the experience at the time of asking. They're not lying when they give you a 9 or 10.

Retention, on the other hand, measures whether someone keeps paying you through difficulty, habit formation, workflow integration, and the accumulated weight of every moment they didn't love the product — including the quiet ones where nothing went wrong but nothing went particularly right either. It asks: does this product win against inertia, competing priorities, budget reviews, and the long tail of mediocre days?

The mechanism that reconciles your numbers is almost certainly one of a few things. The most common is survey timing bias — you're catching people at peak enthusiasm, and the surveys aren't reaching the people who quietly churned or went cold. Churned users don't fill out your NPS surveys. So your 72 is essentially the NPS of your happiest cohort, not your whole user base. This is selection bias baked into the methodology.

The second mechanism is the gap between "I love this" and "I use this every day." Delight and habit are different psychological constructs. People can genuinely rave about a product — recommend it to friends, mean every word of that recommendation — and still not have built the daily or weekly habit that makes them sticky. The product might be wonderful in bursts but not embedded in their workflow. Think about apps you've given 5 stars and then quietly stopped opening.

The third mechanism is that NPS measures the emotional peak, while retention is won or lost in the ordinary moments — the unremarkable Tuesday when someone could easily just not log in, and then doesn't, for two weeks, and then cancels. NPS doesn't capture those moments at all.

The fourth, and often most revealing, possibility is that your product delivers real value in the early months but hits a ceiling. Users genuinely love it at month one or two, tell their friends, fill out surveys enthusiastically, and then around month four or five they've extracted the value it offers and there's no deeper layer to pull them forward. The value is real but it's not compounding. This is a product depth problem, not a satisfaction problem.

What this means practically is that your 72 NPS tells you your product has genuine love in it — that's not nothing, and you shouldn't discount it. But to diagnose the churn you need to stop looking at sentiment and start looking at behavioral signals: what does engagement look like at month 3 and 4 for the cohort that churns at month 6? Are there specific features that retained users use and churned users don't? What's the conversation rate when you do exit interviews with churned users who were previously promoters? That last group is gold — they liked you and left anyway, and they'll usually tell you exactly why if you ask them directly.

The real question isn't "why do people churn despite loving us?" It's "what does loving us not give them, that would make them stay?"
