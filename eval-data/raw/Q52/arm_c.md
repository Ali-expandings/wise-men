## Council answer

The first reframe: you are not deciding where to live for the rest of your life. You are deciding whether to run a 2-3 year financial experiment in a city you find suboptimal. Those are structurally different choices, and conflating them is how people turn a reversible bet into a permanent identity crisis. At 29, no kids, with a partner who is at least nominally flexible, the friction cost of unwinding this in 36 months is low. The cost of not running the experiment is harder to measure but real — compounding works on time, and your late 20s are among the cleanest windows to accelerate.

The math deserves to be made concrete, not left vague. If 40% more means an extra $30-50k/year before tax, and you bank even 25-30% of the delta, you are looking at $20-30k in real accumulated runway over three years — plus the career signal effect, which tends to anchor your next offer upward. "My current comp is just okay" is the sentence that matters here. If you are below market, this may simply be the correction, not a windfall; check that before you over-index on the number. Either way, the delta is only real if you actually bank it. Name a specific savings number — a dollar amount that would meaningfully change your optionality — and make that the definition of success, not the passage of time.

Before deciding anything, audit the "don't want to live there long-term" claim honestly. Is that based on real experience — time spent there, friends who've lived there, genuine knowledge of what daily life looks like — or is it inherited identity, a story about yourself as a person who belongs somewhere else? If you have not spent 3-5 days there as a deliberate audit (not a one-night work trip), do that before signing. The question is not "would I choose this city freely given infinite options" — you wouldn't, fine. The question is "can I tolerate it as a temporary base for 2-3 years without losing something I cannot recover." That is a much lower and more answerable bar.

Do the same audit on what the money is actually for. Write the sentence: "This 40% is specifically for ___, and I will know I have enough when ___." If you cannot complete it cleanly, money is not the actual driver, and you should go looking for the real one — anxiety about peers, a vague need for security, a relationship dynamic, something. If you can complete it and the number is achievable in the time box, the case for going is strong.

The decisive structure: set a concrete shared exit condition with your partner before you go, not after you are unhappy. Something with actual teeth — "if either of us is genuinely miserable at 18 months, we leave regardless of financial progress" — and mean it. This is not a comfort ritual. It is the mechanism that keeps a 2-year bet from becoming a 5-year drift. Without it, you are relying on the implicit assumption that "flexible" will stay flexible under the daily reality of living somewhere unchosen. That assumption is the highest-risk component of this entire decision.

Clear position: take the job, time-boxed to 24-30 months, with a named savings target, an audition visit, and a written shared exit condition that both of you can invoke without it being a relationship rupture. Do not go without those three things in place. The financial opportunity is real, the reversibility is high, and 29/no-kids is close to the optimal window for absorbing exactly this kind of uncomfortable bet.

Confidence: 7.5/10 — the financial and career case is solid, but the binding variable you have not fully disclosed is your partner's actual feeling, not their stated flexibility.

## Dissent worth keeping

"Flexible" is not "happy." Your partner agreed to something they did not initiate and may not have chosen independently. That is a gift, and gifts accrue weight. The relational cost of this move does not arrive as a single dramatic moment — it arrives as a ledger of small daily withdrawals: Sunday evenings in a city that feels like yours but not theirs, sacrifices that go unnamed because they agreed upfront, the slow erosion of the implicit deal that you are building something together rather than one of you servicing the other's career trajectory. Resentment of this kind does not announce itself. It is present before it is named, and by the time it is named it has usually been accumulating for longer than either person realized.

The honest version of the question is not "is my partner flexible enough?" It is: "Can I tolerate this city, alongside a partner who said yes but did not choose, long enough to extract the financial benefit before the relational cost compounds past recovery?" That is a harder question and it requires an honest answer from your partner — not a gracious one. There is a version of this story where you bank the money, hit your number, and leave intact. There is another version where you hit month 14, the city grates on both of you, your partner has absorbed the sacrifice quietly long enough that it has changed something between you, and no savings account fixes that. The dissent's position is not that you should decline. It is that "flexible" is a stated position, not a tested one, and the partner's true feeling — not their generous public answer — is the actual binding constraint on this decision. Get that before anything else.
