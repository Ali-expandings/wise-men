## Answer

The failure mode is almost never the product. It's a combination of three things that hit in sequence:

**1. Press-market fit is mistaken for product-market fit.**
Tech press, Product Hunt, and Hacker News are read by early adopters — a tiny, unrepresentative population who try everything, churn fast, and don't convert to mainstream buyers. A launch spike in signups or downloads gives teams a false signal. The metrics look like traction. They aren't. They're early-adopter curiosity, which is structurally different from mainstream demand.

**2. Launch is the distribution strategy, not the start of one.**
Teams spend 18 months building toward a launch event, then wake up the morning after with no engine. Compounding distribution — SEO moat, word-of-mouth loop, sales motion, integration partnerships, community, content — takes 12-18 months to build and needs to be working before launch so the event accelerates it. Instead, most teams treat launch as the engine itself. When press attention fades (which takes about two weeks), there is no next cohort acquisition mechanism. Cohort 1 is all they ever get.

**3. Activation and retention were never built.**
Even the users who did sign up and had real problems to solve — nobody pulled them back. No onboarding to a success moment, no re-engagement at the right time, no workflow integration, no habit formation. Retention is a product feature. Most teams deprioritize it to get to launch. So even the right users quietly drift away.

The compound result: a spike on a dashboard, a few months of founders trying to recapture the magic with "relaunches," then quiet death. The product was fine. The surrounding system — distribution engine, correct target cohort, retention loops — was never built.

The actionable reframe: a product launch should be an accelerant for systems already running. If the growth engine isn't generating cohort 2 before you launch, the launch changes nothing structurally.

## Dissent worth keeping

**Devil's Advocate:** The launch splash isn't a neutral event followed by mysterious failure — it's actually the cause of death. Too much early attention fills your early cohort with tech enthusiasts whose needs are totally different from your actual TAM. Their feedback corrupts your roadmap toward power-user features. Their usage data paints a false picture of fit. The team raises a seed round on this poisoned cohort, then spends 18 months optimizing for the wrong users. By the time the founders realize they've been talking to the wrong people, the money is gone. The splash killed the company by corrupting the feedback loop at the exact moment it mattered most.
