The metaphor that tends to work best is the **weather forecast**.

Everyone understands that "70% chance of rain" is not a confession of ignorance — it's a precise, useful, actionable statement. It doesn't mean "we have no idea." It doesn't mean "it will definitely rain." It means the forecaster has real information, processed rigorously, that points in a direction while honestly acknowledging the limits of that information. People act on it: they bring an umbrella, they schedule the outdoor wedding with a backup plan. The forecast is doing genuine epistemic work.

The reason this framing succeeds where others fail is that weather forecasts have trained the public to hold two ideas simultaneously: the forecaster is an expert with real knowledge, and the future is genuinely open. These feel like they should be in tension but they aren't. Uncertainty isn't the opposite of knowledge — it's part of how knowledge is expressed when the world is complicated.

You can extend the metaphor. When a climate scientist says "global temperatures will rise 1.5–4.5°C by 2100," that's not a shrug. It's the equivalent of saying "somewhere between a rainy afternoon and a major hurricane is heading toward us." The range itself carries information. A narrower range means better models, more data, stronger constraints. A wider range means genuine physical uncertainty, not sloppy work.

The place weather breaks down is tail risks. People systematically misread low-probability forecasts — a 5% chance of something catastrophic gets treated as "they said it probably won't happen," which isn't quite right. For that, the bridge engineer analogy patches the gap nicely: when a bridge is rated for 10,000 pounds, nobody thinks "the engineer is uncertain about physics." The rating is a precise statement about what's been accounted for, with a built-in safety margin for what hasn't. Safety factors are institutionalized humility, not ignorance.

The deeper reframe worth putting in your paper explicitly: statistical uncertainty is a feature, not a bug. A scientist who gives you a number without an uncertainty range is hiding information from you. The error bars are the honest part. This is the inversion that changes how a general audience reads scientific results — instead of seeing uncertainty as the scientists hedging because they don't really know, they start seeing it as the scientists telling you exactly how much they know and precisely where the limits are.
