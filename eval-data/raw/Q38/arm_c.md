## Council answer

Stop searching for the right metaphor. The oscillation between undersell and oversell is not a craft failure — it is a structural property of metaphor applied to probabilistic reasoning. Every analogy you reach for (weather, archery, gambling) has a different epistemic structure than a statistical claim, and readers import that structure wholesale. The most dangerous example: the weather-forecast metaphor trains readers to expect a resolution date. "Did it rain?" has a clean answer. Parameter estimates, sampling distributions, and model-assumption uncertainty often do not. A reader who absorbs the weather framing will ask "so when will we know for sure?" — a question your domain may permanently refuse to answer. You have made comprehension harder, not easier.

The primary recommendation is worked frequency numbers, not a metaphor:

"In a sample of 1,000 people, we estimated X. If we repeated this study 100 times with different random samples, about 95 of those repetitions would produce an estimate between A and B."

That is harder to read for two seconds. It does not lie. Pair it with one direct plain-English sentence — not a figure, a statement — of what the evidence licenses and what it does not: "The data are strong enough to conclude X; they are not strong enough to conclude Y." State the evidence base first, the range second. Uncertainty that follows demonstrated evidence reads as honesty; uncertainty stated first reads as a hedge.

One refinement from the Editor's framing: distinguish precision (how tight the range) from robustness (how much the range shifts if assumptions change). These are different claims and collapse at different rates under scrutiny. Know which one your finding actually involves before you write about it.

If after all this you still want a figurative anchor, the salvage conditions are strict. Use a single calibrated metaphor — weather-with-a-track-record is the least broken option — but it is not functional without what the Audience Advocate identifies as the mandatory contrast beat: an explicit sentence that names and then dismisses the wrong interpretation. "This doesn't mean the scientists are guessing. It means they are being precise about what the data can and cannot show." Without that second move, the metaphor does exactly the harm described above. The contrast beat is the step most writers skip; skipping it is why most science-communication metaphors backfire. Never deploy a bare metaphor.

If using the weather framing, the Rhetorician's supplement is load-bearing: add a frequency restatement ("imagine this forecast issued 100 times") to block the binary-outcome strip where readers reduce "70% chance" to "probably yes." One metaphor does not carry two opposing cognitive loads simultaneously; state both explicitly.

## Dissent worth keeping

This is an explicit inversion: the conventional craft position is the dissent here, held by four of five members, and it should be stated at full strength.

The best science communicators do not abandon figurative language — they use multiple short metaphors in sequence, each doing narrow work, none asked to carry the full conceptual load alone. The Audience Advocate's two-move structure (process metaphor, then contrast beat) has a demonstrated track record: framing uncertainty as epistemic integrity rather than epistemic failure changes how readers relate to the finding, not just how they understand it. The Rhetorician's paired weather-plus-dead-reckoning addresses both faces of the problem — calibrated honesty about limits, and the action-oriented posture of acting on best available inference without paralysis. These are not decorative; they do cognitive work that frequency tables alone do not.

The Devil's Advocate's worked-numbers recommendation assumes that general readers of research papers can handle numerical explanation without figurative scaffolding. That assumption holds for a narrow slice of the audience. For the broader general audience the paper is aimed at, metaphor is not a luxury — it is the mechanism by which abstract probabilistic concepts become memorable and transferable outside the moment of reading. The failure case the DA identifies (wrong intuitions imported) is real, but the solution is better metaphor architecture, not metaphor abandonment.

Four members hold this position. It is the mainstream of science communication practice. Treat the DA's recommendation as the floor — the minimum that doesn't mislead — and the four-member position as the ceiling worth building toward if you have the craft to execute it cleanly.
