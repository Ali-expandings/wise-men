## Council answer

The fear is real but substantially overblown for someone with a 10-year record. To answer the empirical question directly: a 3-6 month gap causes manageable friction — a modestly longer search, some early-funnel resume screens, occasional interview framing work — not a wall. Your decade of compounding experience is the dominant signal on a resume; a gap is noise by comparison. The COVID years and the 2022-2024 mass layoff cycle normalized career pauses to the point where most recruiters read a 3-6 month gap as sabbatical, personal reset, or layoff, not as inability to handle pressure. The "can't handle pressure" inference is the more serious of your two fears, and it lives almost entirely in framing, not in the gap itself.

Unbundle the two fears because they need different responses. "Unhireable" is a logistics problem: some managers filter you at the resume stage, so you need a stronger narrative to convert — it lengthens the funnel, it doesn't close the door. "Can't handle pressure" is an inference that screeners draw, and you control it. One concrete test (Advisor D): write down three specific roles you'd be rejected from *solely* because of the gap. If you can't name them with specifics, the fear isn't tracking a real object — it may be a proxy for the harder question of what you actually want from the next decade.

The real risk is not the gap — it is re-entry drift. Six months becomes eighteen when you lose momentum, income runway compresses, and identity dissolves without a forcing function. Guard against it structurally: set a hard return date before you leave, not after. Write and test your one-sentence narrative now, while you still have context — "I had ten years of strong execution, recognized I was running on empty, and made a deliberate choice to recover before the next chapter" is a complete, adult sentence that closes the conversation. Vagueness and apology are what create doubt, not the gap itself.

Execute the break in two phases. Months one and two: genuine rest, no resume-filling, no "productive sabbatical" content creation — actually decompress, because the aliveness you feel when you genuinely want to work again is visible in interviews and is worth more than any line item. Months three to four: light network update (one or two touchpoints per month), a skills currency audit (fast-moving niches like AI infrastructure may need 2-4 weeks of catch-up, most others do not), and clarity on what you want next. Map your target market by company tier before you return: at Series B and under startups, a gap is essentially irrelevant; at FAANG or staff-plus tracks, one small visible project during the break (a post, an OSS commit) neutralizes most screening risk. The people who would reject you outright for a deliberate recovery gap are, in all likelihood, running the culture that burned you out.

## Dissent worth keeping

The reassuring consensus above was largely formed during 2021-2022, when companies were desperate and sabbaticals became normalized by necessity. The 2024-2026 market is structurally different: mass layoffs created a buyer's market, ATS filters cull aggressively before human eyes see your record, and a manager under time pressure defaults to the no-gap candidate when the pile is tall. The risks the consensus underweights are real: returning into a worse market after months without income weakens your negotiating position; AI tooling is moving fast enough that 6 months is a genuine competency gap in affected roles, not just a perceived one; burnout that is structural — rooted in the industry, career trajectory, or identity — will not resolve in 3-6 months, meaning the gap can become an open-ended 9-12 month hole; and network decay is costly because referrals remain the highest-yield re-entry channel and they require maintenance.

The sharper play, if health permits any flexibility at all, is a targeted transition rather than a hard stop: leave the current role, take 4-6 weeks of genuine rest, then move to contract or fractional work at 10-15 hours per week. This keeps the resume live, reads to a screener as "consulting" — a materially different signal than "gap" — and preserves optionality. If a full stop is genuinely necessary for health, treat 3 months as the defensible ceiling, not 6. The gap-is-fine consensus is not wrong; it is potentially calibrated to a market that no longer exists at the same temperature.
