The honest answer is: you almost certainly cannot beat them head-on, and you should not try. With 10x resources and a strong brand, they will outspend you on every front where the competition is symmetric.

The playbook that actually works against a dominant incumbent has a few proven paths, and the right one depends on your specific situation.

Finding the niche they ignore is usually the strongest first move. Incumbents with strong brands are optimized for their core customer. They have organizational gravity — sales teams, support processes, product roadmaps — all tuned to the majority segment. Niches feel small to them and get deprioritized. But a niche that's small for them can be your entire viable business. The key is picking a niche where their generic solution is genuinely a bad fit, not just slightly worse. If you can become the obvious choice for that specific segment, you own it and they can't easily dislodge you without alienating their core.

Going after unhappy customers is real but riskier than it sounds. Unhappy customers are unhappy for a reason, and sometimes that reason is that they're just hard to serve. You need to understand why they're unhappy — if it's because the incumbent doesn't care enough about their use case, that's structural and exploitable. If it's because they have unusual demands that are expensive to meet, you're just inheriting someone else's problem. The other risk: the incumbent can notice you poaching and fix the issue. If their churn is low-cost to stop, you don't have a durable wedge.

The third path — and often the best one — is doing something the dominant player structurally cannot do because of who they are. Their size, their existing customers, their business model, their brand positioning all constrain them. They can't suddenly be cheap if they're premium. They can't move fast if they have enterprise contracts. They can't be personal if they're scaled. You look for the thing that their success makes impossible for them to offer, then build your identity around exactly that. This isn't just finding a gap — it's finding a gap that stays a gap because filling it would hurt them.

The worst thing you can do is position as "the better version of them for everyone." That's where resources win. You need an initial foothold where you can win unconditionally, generate revenue and proof, then expand from strength. The goal in year one is not to beat them — it's to own something they don't care about yet, until you're big enough that it matters.
