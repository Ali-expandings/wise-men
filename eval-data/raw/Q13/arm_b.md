## Answer

Neither "unhappy customers" nor "niche" is the right frame on its own — the real question is: where is the dominant player structurally constrained, not just product-weak?

A 10x resource advantage and strong brand mean they can copy features, outspend you on acquisition, and wait you out in most head-to-head fights. But they cannot easily change their business model, their cost structure, or their culture. That's your opening.

The move: find the dimension of the market where their constraints are your advantages. Classic examples — they can't go downmarket without destroying margin, can't serve a specific vertical deeply without alienating their horizontal base, can't adopt a new delivery model without breaking existing contracts. Their brand is built around something specific; anything outside that specific thing is structural drag for them, not just product backlog.

How to execute:
1. Use unhappy customers as research and initial distribution, not as strategy. Their complaints tell you where the structural pain is. And they're already motivated to look, so they're your fastest early adopters. But "we serve unhappy customers of X" is not a durable position — you need to know why the dominant player can't fix those problems.
2. Pick the niche where your smallness is a feature, not a compromise. Speed, specialization, willingness to do things that don't scale, deep vertical integration — things the dominant player's overhead makes impossible.
3. Win that niche completely. Not "present in," not "competitive in" — dominant. Case studies, word of mouth, the only name that comes up when people in that slice search.
4. Design the niche with expansion logic from day one. The niche should be a beachhead into adjacencies, not a permanent ceiling.

The filter for choosing: is this segment ignored because the dominant player can't serve it (structural), or just hasn't gotten to it yet (product backlog)? Only the former is durable. If it's just backlog, they'll get there and you're fighting a clock.

## Dissent worth keeping

"Niche-hiding can mean small and irrelevant forever. The biggest disruptions — Slack, Notion, Basecamp — went straight at the dominant player's core value prop with a demonstrably better experience. They didn't hide in corners no one cared about. If your product is genuinely better, going after the dominant player's most vocal unhappy customers generates word-of-mouth, case studies, and momentum faster than quietly building in a niche nobody's watching. 'Find a niche they ignore' is safe advice that produces safe, mediocre outcomes."
