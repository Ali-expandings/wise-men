## Answer

Do not chase unhappy customers — that is the most common move and the most punished one. The winning approach is to find the structural wedge: a segment, delivery model, or use-case framing the incumbent cannot serve without cannibalizing their core business. Concretely, spend two weeks mapping what the incumbent's business model must protect (margin floors, enterprise deal sizes, channel relationships, pricing architecture) and enter precisely where defending against you costs them more than ignoring you. The niche must be structurally unattractive to them — too small, too awkward, too compliance-heavy, too different in delivery — not merely underserved. Own that niche so completely (80%+ share within 18 months) that you have a defensible base before they react. Name a world, not a comparison: position as the only thing that exists for a specific type of customer, not as a cheaper or friendlier version of the incumbent.

## Dissent worth keeping

The Devil's Advocate position, rated highest on insight across the council: both options on the table — unhappy customers and ignored niches — are reactive traps that hand the incumbent your strategic map. Unhappy customers are a known playbook; the incumbent has seen it before and has the resources to match and outlast you. Ignored niches are often ignored for a reason: they are unprofitable dead-ends that do not scale. The real play is to attack from a direction that makes their strengths irrelevant — a categorically different delivery model, pricing structure, or use-case framing they cannot copy without destroying the core business they are built to protect. Your asymmetric advantages are speed and irreversibility. The question is not "where are the gaps in their existing game?" but "what axis of competition did they structurally opt out of by becoming what they are?" Find that axis and own it before they can respond.
