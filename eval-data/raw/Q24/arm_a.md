Generalize cautiously, and here's the reasoning:

The effect is probably real but the magnitude is uncertain. An 80-person RCT is small enough that the 12-point estimate has wide confidence intervals — the true effect could be meaningfully larger or smaller. Replications in larger samples could easily show 6 points or 18 points.

The demographic skew matters a lot for cholesterol specifically. Women 40-55 are perimenopausal, a period when lipid profiles are actively changing due to estrogen shifts. The intervention may work through mechanisms that are hormonally modulated, so effects in men, younger women, or postmenopausal women could differ substantially. This isn't just a "we didn't sample everyone" statistical problem — there's a biological reason to expect heterogeneity.

Single-city recruitment is a real constraint. Participants share geography, which correlates with baseline diet, food access, ethnicity distribution, and possibly genetic background. If the intervention involves replacing certain foods, what's available and affordable in that city shapes compliance and substitution patterns that won't replicate everywhere.

Eight weeks is short. Cholesterol responds to diet relatively quickly, but 8-week effects don't tell you about 6-month or 2-year adherence or whether the lipid changes persist. Many diet RCTs show early gains that attenuate.

The "all-women, 40-55" and "one city" constraints are the binding ones here. I'd be comfortable saying: "This diet likely has a real cholesterol-lowering effect in middle-aged women, probably in the single digits to low teens of LDL points, under conditions where people actually follow it." I would not generalize to men, younger adults, populations with different baseline diets, or assume the effect size holds precisely. I'd treat it as preliminary signal worth testing in a larger, more diverse trial rather than as a definitive effect estimate to plug into population-level calculations.
