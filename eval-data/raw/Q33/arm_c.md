## Council answer

The framing of "writing problem vs. culture problem" is a false binary, and it is probably the wrong question. The more useful reframe is this: your goal should not be "people read docs" — it should be "people get accurate answers quickly without creating human bottlenecks." Once you hold that goal instead, the writing-vs-culture distinction becomes a diagnostic question, not a moral one, and the answer changes what you fix.

Before doing anything else, run a cheap two-week diagnostic to actually separate the two failure modes. For every question that a doc should have answered, record the exact phrasing the person used when they asked it. Then take a colleague who did not write the doc — someone who would count as a naive reader — and give them only that phrasing. Ask them to find the relevant section in under 90 seconds using whatever the team normally uses to search. Alternatively, when someone asks a doc-answerable question in chat, reply by asking them to first find and post the relevant doc section along with a note on what it did not answer. Both approaches give you the same clean fork: if people cannot find the answer, you have a writing and information-architecture problem; if they find it readily but did not look in the first place, you have a culture and habit problem.

The tells for each failure mode are distinct. A writing and discoverability problem shows up as: the naive colleague searches using the asker's phrasing and lands on the wrong page, or lands nowhere, or lands in the right document but cannot locate the relevant section within 90 seconds. This is what the Editor calls the author-illusion — "well-organized and accurate" is a judgment the author makes relative to how they think about the material, not relative to how a stressed colleague with a half-formed question arrives at it cold. The fix here is not prose polish. It is search, navigation, and cold-start usability: better titles that match the language people actually use when confused, shorter entry points, and anchor links that get someone to the answer without requiring them to read the whole document first.

A culture and habit problem shows up as: the naive colleague finds the section easily, but the original asker never tried. This is the Rhetorician's diagnosis — asking a colleague is faster, warmer, status-safe, and carries no risk of looking foolish for misreading something. That calculus does not change when the prose gets cleaner. The fix is to reduce the cost of the right path, not to increase the virtue required to walk it. The most durable structural intervention is to wire your documentation into the channel where questions get asked — a bot that surfaces the relevant section before a human can respond removes the social shortcut without requiring anyone to change their habits voluntarily. Leaders modeling the behavior matters too: when someone senior publicly redirects a question to a doc, links to it, and visibly submits a correction when something is out of date, it signals that consulting docs is normal rather than pedantic. Reward the people who update docs, not just the people who write them.

One risk cuts across both failure modes and deserves special attention: staleness and trust. A single experience of following a doc to a wrong answer — acting on outdated information — can anchor avoidance behavior for months or years. If your team's unspoken prior is "the docs might be wrong," no amount of discoverability or nudging will overcome it. Part of your diagnostic should include whether people say things like "I wasn't sure if it was current" — that is its own signal, and the fix is a visible freshness indicator and a lightweight norm around flagging stale content, not more writing.

Confidence: high that the diagnostic approach correctly separates the two failure modes; medium that any single fix is sufficient, because most teams with unread docs have both problems layered on each other and need to work both sides simultaneously.

## Dissent worth keeping

The premise of the question conceals a failure of diagnosis in the other direction: "nobody reads the docs" may be evidence of a rational system functioning correctly, not a dysfunction that needs correction. Asking a colleague takes thirty seconds and returns a contextualized, personalized, uncertainty-flagged answer from someone who knows the team's current situation. Reading a doc takes five minutes and may still leave a gap that requires a follow-up question anyway. The equilibrium in which people ask rather than read is the market-clearing price of synchronous human knowledge transfer simply beating asynchronous written transfer on the dimensions that matter to the person with the question — speed, confidence, and social safety.

The right response to this is not to make the docs better or to nudge behavior. It is to stop fighting the equilibrium. Audit the existing documentation honestly: how many of those documents have ever changed a decision, prevented a repeated mistake, or served a reader who could not have gotten the answer from a person? Archive everything that fails that test. For the survivors, stop treating the doc as the primary artifact that people are supposed to consult proactively. Instead, make the doc the exhaust of the question-answering culture: when someone answers a repeated question in chat, that answer lives in the channel where everyone can see it, and the doc is linked as the receipts — the auditable, correctable record of what was said. Docs become downstream of conversation, not upstream of it. The question-answering behavior the team already has becomes the engine that keeps documentation alive and current, rather than a competing behavior that documentation is constantly losing to.
