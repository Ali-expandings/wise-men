## Recommendation

Neither half of the question is quite right, and the more useful third answer is a domain-general statistical mechanism, not a special property of social science. A "robust-seeming" psychology effect usually earned that reputation by clearing a significance filter — via publication bias, researcher degrees of freedom, or outright p-hacking, which sit on one continuum rather than being separate explanations — while resting on a verbal theory that predicted only a direction, not a size. That combination mechanically manufactures inflated, poorly-replicating literatures even when no one involved did anything they'd call cheating. A comparable failure pattern reportedly shows up in fields with none of psychology's alleged interpretive mushiness (unverified — see Notes on unverified figures), which argues against "social phenomena are just too context-bound to be law-like" as the deeper story. But there's a genuine, evidence-backed case that this statistical account, while true, is itself the symptom of an unaddressed institutional incentive problem — see Strongest counter-position.

## Why

A p-value under a verbal theory ("X increases Y") is a weak test: it licenses only a model-conditional claim about a family of alternatives, never belief in the specific point estimate obtained, and a directionally-correct result barely discriminates the theory from its rivals. When only results that clear a significance threshold get published, or get analyzed and re-analyzed until they do, the surviving estimates are a selected sample — and selecting on a noisy statistic exceeding a threshold mechanically inflates it (a "winner's curse" / regression-to-the-mean effect, described in meta-science work on selection-driven inflation) (unverified — check the specific citations). This happens whether or not any individual researcher consciously manipulated anything: chronically low statistical power across psychology for decades, combined with publish-if-significant filtering, is sufficient on its own.

This is why "seemed very robust" is close to the wrong signal to trust. A pile of small, independently-published significant studies, or a large positive meta-analytic effect size, mostly indicates that many attempts passed through the same filter — not that the effect is real at the claimed size. A run of studies each individually underpowered that nonetheless all land significant is itself a mild warning sign, not reassurance (unverified — check the specific probability math and example study count). Failed replications aren't old sins being exposed; they're what this statistical setup predicts in advance for a non-trivial share of "robust" findings, and several lines of evidence — large multi-lab replication projects, a gap in positive-result rates between studies registered before results are known and standard publications, and a similar collapse pattern reportedly appearing outside psychology — point the same direction (all unverified — see Notes on unverified figures below).

Two things complicate a clean "it's just the statistics" verdict, and both matter for how much to trust any specific finding. First, pure selection mechanics predicts shrinkage toward a smaller but still-real effect on replication, not necessarily complete evaporation to indistinguishable-from-zero; when an effect vanishes entirely under a well-powered retest, that points either to an original result that was a fluke or aggressively hacked, or to a real but narrower effect whose boundary conditions the original verbal theory never specified — telling these apart requires case-by-case diagnostics, not a general rule. Second, the pattern is uneven: effects that are more cognitive/perceptual and less socially or motivationally loaded reportedly replicate at meaningfully higher rates than the reverse (unverified), which is more consistent with "these particular subfields had smaller effects, looser theory, and lower power" than with "psychology as a whole is unknowable."

## What to do

A usable triage for any specific "robust" finding, in rough priority order:

1. Check whether a large-sample, preregistered direct replication or Registered Report already exists (a replication-database search or literature check, well under an hour) — trust that number over a count of original significant studies or a standard meta-analytic estimate.
2. If the claim rests mainly on a meta-analysis rather than one strong test, check whether it has been re-run with corrections for publication bias (selection models, PET-PEESE-type adjustments) — an uncorrected pooled estimate can look robust purely because of what never got published.
3. If no direct replication exists, check whether the original was preregistered and analyzed as planned, with achieved statistical power (and basic error-checks such as statcheck or GRIM) against the effect size claimed. A preregistered original takes p-hacking off the table for that study and narrows the field to power, theory, measurement, and whatever sibling or unpublished studies surround it.
4. Check whether the underlying theory predicts a magnitude or boundary condition at all, or only a direction — a theory that can't be wrong about size can't really be tested by one significant result — and check measurement quality directly: did manipulation checks pass, and do the measures have established validity, since a confound or a demand effect can replicate exactly as cleanly as a real effect.
5. Before trusting a specific replication's failure or success, check whether it was adequately powered to detect a plausible fraction of the original effect, used naive participants, and matched the original materials, population, and any author-endorsed protocol — and whether its result is a genuine null (a tight interval around zero) or merely inconclusive.

This is the same test regardless of whether the deeper cause in a given case turns out to be statistical, institutional, or genuinely about context-dependence — it's what would have to be true for any of the three explanations to be the right one for that specific finding.

## Risks of this plan

- Treating "this is mechanically expected, not fraud" as "therefore fine": an inflated, misleading literature does real damage — to theses built on it, practitioners who apply it, and public trust — regardless of whether anyone involved meant to deceive.
- Over-crediting the statistical/methodological account risks exactly what the counter-position below warns about: methods reforms (preregistration, Registered Reports) can be adopted cosmetically by journals, universities, and funders while the underlying reward structure for splashy findings continues unchanged.
- Under-crediting it risks the opposite error: assuming better incentives alone would fully close the replication gap, when selection and low power would still distort estimates even under much better researcher incentives, just less severely (unverified — the size of that residual gap is contested and worth checking against current Registered-Report data).
- Every specific figure behind this explanation (replication rates, effect-size shrinkage, the cross-field parallel, the Registered-Report gap) was recalled rather than freshly checked, and they cluster around a small set of well-known flagship studies rather than fully independent sources — a correction to any one of them could shift the picture for several of the claims above at once, not just one.
- Treating any single failed or successful replication as dispositive for "the field" overstates what one data point can show; the honest picture is a distribution that varies by subfield, original power, and original effect size, not a single verdict.

Cost of being wrong: nothing here is a one-way door. The triage above is cheap (mostly literature-checking) and reversible, and the two explanations in tension mainly disagree about where to aim reform effort (methods vs. institutions) rather than about what to do with any single specific finding today — the recovery path either way is the same: prefer the preregistered, adequately powered, replicated version of a claim over the original, and update if a better-run version disagrees.

## Strongest counter-position

The case that this is fundamentally an incentives problem, not a statistics problem: psychology, and the fields around it, effectively ran a market for findings. At nearly every stage — researcher, journal editor, university press office, funder, textbook author, science journalist, the conference-and-consulting circuit — a surprising positive result was rewarded, and checking it almost never was. A false positive paid off immediately for its author; the cost landed later, on the students who built on it, the practitioners who applied it, and the field's credibility generally. On this view, low power, unfalsifiable verbal theory, and flexible analysis aren't the root cause — they're what a market like that predictably produces and tolerates, because rigor was never what it paid for. Treating this as a methods problem is the comfortable diagnosis: it doesn't indict any particular institution, it lets the authors of specific failed findings point to hidden moderators or unfaithful replicators, and it protects the field's status — whereas the institutional account puts responsibility on journals, universities, and funders who would have to change what they reward. It wins if formats that remove the reward for a surprising result — studies a journal accepts before the results are known — turn out to produce dramatically different (lower false-positive, more modest, more stable) results than the standard published literature; a persistent, large gap of exactly that kind reportedly already exists (unverified — see Notes on unverified figures), which would be direct evidence for this position rather than a hypothetical.

## Confidence

High: the question's own framing treats "p-hacking" and "something deeper about social science" as mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive, and they're neither. Low power, publication bias, and researcher degrees of freedom sit on the same continuum as deliberate p-hacking rather than being a separate, shallower cause, and that continuum mechanically produces this exact failure pattern without anyone needing to have decided to cheat — so "just p-hacking, retroactively exposed" undersells the mechanism. This is close to a logical/structural point about the mechanism rather than a contested empirical claim.

Medium: that the dominant proximate mechanism is a selection-and-power-driven "winner's curse" operating largely independent of deliberate misconduct. The logic is close to deductively sound, but how much of any specific literature's failure to replicate is this mechanism versus outright questionable research practices versus genuine, real context-dependence is case-specific and isn't resolved here in general form.

Low-to-medium, and genuinely contested: whether the statistical mechanism above or the institutional-incentive account (see counter-position) is doing more of the causal work. These aren't fully independent explanations — incentives could be what produces the statistical pattern rather than compete with it — but they point to different priorities for reform (methods vs. institutions), and the case made here doesn't cleanly settle which deserves more weight.

Material unknowns: almost every specific magnitude cited above (replication rates, effect-size shrinkage, the cross-field parallel, the Registered-Report positive-result gap) was not checked against a primary source — see below. Separately, the statistical-mechanism explanation rests on a common, fairly small pool of flagship studies and projects rather than on fully independent strands of evidence, so it should be treated as one well-supported account resting on a handful of data points, not as several independent confirmations of the same conclusion.
