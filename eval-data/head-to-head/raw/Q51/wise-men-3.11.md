## Recommendation

Before treating this as a choice between the outside offer and the manager track, spend the next one to two weeks converting your current employer's revealed interest in you into a written senior or staff-level IC step — or, if you genuinely want to test management, a time-boxed manager trial with a guaranteed return date — using the outside offer's timeline as the reason to ask now. If that written step doesn't materialize and the smaller company's stability and equity terms hold up under a direct look, take the senior IC offer over the manager track as currently described.

## Why

Both roles on the table have a structural problem neither company is going to name for you. The outside role is a *senior* offer — lateral in level, not a promotion — at a company whose stability you haven't yet verified. The internal option is a *track*, not a role: a direction with no confirmed level, comp, team, or return path attached. Neither is automatically the "strategic" move your question is worried about; a title alone isn't strategy.

What you've written points at the real signal. "I like coding more than meetings" is a direct, first-person statement about what engages you. "I feel like I should be more strategic" carries no content behind it — no comp pressure named, no team you want to build, no specific part of the manager job you're drawn to. That asymmetry matters: wanting the *work* of managing (hiring, unblocking people, owning ambiguity at the org level) is what makes a first-time manager stick; wanting the title because it looks like growth is the profile most likely to reverse back to IC within about two years (asserted pattern from career-coaching experience — unverified against your specific employer).

None of this is a function of being 32 specifically. The actual constraint on a later management on-ramp is accumulated tenure and trust at one employer, not age — first-time managers in their late 30s take on the role successfully often enough that 32 on its own is not a deadline (asserted pattern — unverified against your specific company's promotion norms). What tenure does mean is that the choice in front of you is about which clock you want to keep running, not about beating an age cutoff.

That's why the asking move matters more than the pick. A company with a real engineering ladder usually separates deep technical ownership from people management for exactly this reason — the "good engineer pushed into management" mismatch is common enough that most have already built the off-ramp. If your company has that ladder, or will build it under pressure from a competing offer, it beats both options as posed: same institutional trust and stability you've already built, no forced people-management, no company-risk reset. If it doesn't — and some companies genuinely won't create one, or will treat the ask as disloyalty rather than information — that refusal is itself real information, and it's cheaper to get that answer now, while you have a second offer, than later without one.

If it does come down to the literal binary, the fallback favors the IC offer, on the evidence in front of us: your own stated preference is unambiguous, the "should" carries no named substance, and the specific test that separates a manager role worth taking from one worth declining is simple — can you name three concrete things about the work of managing itself (not the title, not the org chart position) that you actually want? If yes, a management role is worth a real trial, ideally time-boxed with the return path written down before you start, since a short stint is comparatively cheap to reverse and a long one with no shipped work is not. If you can't name those three things, that's the answer, and it points at the IC path.

One real counterargument survives this and belongs in your decision, not just in a footnote: leaving for the outside offer is the only option here that resets your accumulated trust at your current employer to zero — the capital that currently lets people say yes to you without a meeting — in exchange for a lateral title, probably less comp, and more concentration risk at a company you haven't yet verified is more stable than the one you'd be leaving. See "Strongest counter-position" below before you decide the fallback question.

## What to do

Get the outside offer's deadline in writing and ask for a one-to-two week extension — reasonable requests are usually granted, and a flat refusal is itself signal about that company.

Within the extension window, have one direct conversation with your manager or skip-level and get three answers in writing: (1) what exact level, title, and comp does the "manager track" land at, and effective when; (2) does a senior/staff/principal IC ladder exist, what's the bar, and would you be on a path to it; (3) if you tried management and it wasn't right, what's the documented path back to IC and at what level. A company that won't answer (1) and (3) in writing is offering you a workload, not a promotion — treat a non-answer as a decision point, not a delay.

In parallel, run the smaller company's stability and equity checklist. Stability: current headcount versus a year ago, last funding round and runway, and why the role is open. Equity — the specifics that most often turn an attractive-looking number into worth nothing: strike price and current valuation (409A), your share against the fully-diluted total, the liquidation-preference stack and multiple, and the post-termination exercise window (90 days is standard and can force a costly decision; some companies extend it). On your own side, check what unvested equity you'd forfeit by the timing of a move, and read your existing agreement's non-compete/non-solicit terms yourself rather than assuming a rule of thumb. Then do the diligence step almost nobody does: talk to one or two people who left the smaller company in the last year, and separately, someone at your current company who became a first-time manager under your chain and — if one exists — someone who went from manager back to IC there. Their answers, not the funding press release or the org chart, are the real stability and return-path test.

Run the "three concrete things" test on yourself honestly: what, specifically, about managing people do you want? If you can name three, a management trial is worth taking — ideally time-boxed at roughly 18-24 months, with the return-path commitment above written down before you start. If you want a harder test than your own introspection, the cheap version is to actually run one hiring loop as the hiring manager and write one real performance review, end to end — a matter of hours, not months, and more honest than picturing the job in the abstract.

Decide on a set date, not by drift: take the manager track only if it clears the written commitments above and you passed your own test; take the IC offer if the internal ladder doesn't exist or won't be created and the smaller company's diligence — equity mechanics included — checks out; and if neither clears, that itself is information, worth a broader look rather than defaulting to whichever door is easiest to walk through this month.

## Risks of this plan

Asking for scope or a written return path can read as flight-risk signaling — most workplaces treat it as a legitimate negotiation once a real outside offer exists, but a few will start a clock instead; you won't know which kind you're in until you ask.

If the internal IC ladder turns out not to exist and never will, you've spent part of your extension window finding that out rather than deciding — budget it as a cost worth paying, not a failure of the plan.

Taking the manager role for the title rather than the work is the single most common regret pattern here — reluctant managers often reverse back to IC within about two years (asserted pattern, unverified against your specific case), and whether that reversal is cheap or costly depends on whether the stint was time-boxed with a written return path from the start, not on how long you waited to decide.

Taking the IC offer resets your internal tenure and trust to zero at exactly the moment that trust is what most future management or leadership opportunities would otherwise be built on internally; "slightly smaller" also means real, if moderate, added exposure to layoffs, reorgs, or acquisition disrupting the role, and any equity in the offer should be valued at both zero and at plan — never assumed as realized comp until the preference stack and exercise window are checked.

The cost of waiting is real but small and bounded: one to two weeks against an offer you can ask to extend, versus multiple years inside whichever path turns out to be the wrong one. If this plan turns out wrong — you take the written IC step or the time-boxed management trial and it still doesn't fit — the recovery path is the same either way: both routes were deliberately built to leave a documented way back, which is the whole point of insisting on it in writing before you start.

## Strongest counter-position

The case for not taking the IC offer, and for testing management now rather than deferring it: your largest non-transferable asset at 32 is the trust you've already built at your current employer — the kind that lets people say yes to you without a meeting. The outside IC offer is the one option on the table that sets that asset to zero, paying a real price for no title, probably little comp, and more concentration risk, in exchange for the same level at a company you haven't yet verified is more stable than the one you'd be leaving. Your stated preference for coding over meetings is data about the last two years, not about management — you've never done the job, so it's a prediction, not an observation, and it's the most commonly falsified prediction in exactly this kind of decision. And a single outside offer, from a single process, is a sample size of one; treating it as a market verdict on your value overweights it.

This view wins if the manager role turns out to carry real scope and a genuine written return path, and if you can, in fact, name concrete things about the management work itself that you want once you're forced to articulate them rather than default to "should." It loses if the track is open-ended with no return commitment, or if it turns out to be a retention gesture rather than a real promotion — in which case the trust it asks you to spend was never going to be honored anyway.
