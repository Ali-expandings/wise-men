## Council Verdict: Senior IC Offer vs. Manager Track

### Where the Council Agrees

Four points surfaced independently across most or all five advisors, before anyone saw anyone else's work.

The "should be more strategic" line is doing too much work. Every advisor who engaged with it directly — Contrarian, First Principles, Outsider, Executor — flagged the same thing: it's a felt pressure, not a tested belief. Nobody said it to them. No review flagged it. It's a script about what 32 is supposed to look like, sitting next to an actual lived data point: you already know you like coding more than meetings. The council's near-unanimous read is that the tested preference should outweigh the untested narrative.

The IC-vs-manager binary is false. Both the Contrarian and the First Principles Thinker named this directly, and nothing else in the council disputed it: "strategic" doesn't mean "manages people." Staff, Principal, and Distinguished IC tracks carry real scope and organizational leverage with zero direct reports. If the word "strategic" is what's pulling you toward Option B, that itch has a technical answer, not just a managerial one.

Reversibility is not symmetric. Contrarian, Outsider, Executor, and Expansionist all independently landed on the same shape of argument: staying hands-on now preserves the option to move into management later, credibly — the best managers are usually ICs people already respected. Going into management now and clawing back deep technical currency later is a harder trip, in a field where eighteen months off the tools is a real gap.

Option A is real; Option B isn't, yet. Outsider and Executor both point out the asymmetry in what's actually on the table: A is a dated, signed offer. B is a "track" — no defined scope, no committed timeline, no manager who's said what you'd own or when. Treating them as two equally solid options understates how much uncertainty is baked into B.

### Where the Council Clashes

Two disagreements survived peer review — not errors, genuine differences in how to weigh the same facts.

Is "smaller company" a risk or an asset? The Expansionist argues smaller means more scope per engineer, a faster path to de facto tech lead, and promotion on shipping rather than politics — turning what everyone else treats as a quiet downside into the actual upside case for Option A. But this is also where the rest of the council pushed back hardest: four of five peer reviews flagged that this case leans on unverified claims — "2026 AI-augmented ICs are the highest-leverage unit," comp "rivaling VP pay" — asserted as fact, with no price put on the real downside of smaller employers: runway, funding risk, layoff exposure. This is the one load-bearing disagreement in the whole council, and the prompt gives no data to settle it either way.

Does concreteness count as evidence? The Executor's "Monday-morning test" favors Option A because it's actionable by Friday, while Option B is still a conversation waiting to happen. The Expansionist's review calls this activation-energy bias dressed up as rigor — favoring whichever path is shovel-ready isn't the same as favoring whichever path is better, especially for a decision explicitly framed as a multi-year fork. Both sides have a point: A being verified and B being speculative is real information, not just bias. But it's fair to ask whether "easy to start" quietly stood in for "correct" somewhere in that reasoning.

### Blind Spots the Council Caught

The peer review round surfaced things no individual response landed on cleanly.

The single biggest miss, and the most-corroborated finding of the exercise: three of five reviewers, reasoning independently under anonymity, named the exact same move that none of the five original responses made central — take the outside offer back to current leadership and use it as leverage to negotiate a Staff or Principal IC track, instead of accepting "leave" or "become a manager" as the only two doors. A fourth arrived at the same place from a different angle: verify, don't infer — ask current leadership the direct question this week, and ask the new company for a short extension while you do. All five advisor responses accepted the employer's binary as fixed. The reviewers, looking at the set side by side, didn't.

The response most reviewers rated strongest also turned out to be the most cross-validated, not just the best-argued: the First Principles Thinker's reframing — the real question isn't A-or-B, it's whether a senior-IC ladder exists above your current level, and the fact that management is what got offered instead is itself the signal — was independently picked as strongest by three of five reviewers, including two judging it with nothing to gain by praising it.

The same review caught something none of the advisor responses noticed in their own text: they all treat "dislikes meetings" as evidence against management without distinguishing IC meetings (status, reviews) from manager meetings (1:1s, unblocking, conflict). Nobody in the room actually has data on the second kind — the stated preference is thinner evidence against Option B specifically than it looks.

One more, smaller catch worth naming: the Contrarian's own opening move contained a factual slip, describing Option B as "changing employers," when B is explicitly the stay option. The Outsider's review caught it. The underlying point survives the correction, but it's a clean example of the review pass doing real work rather than rubber-stamping.

And one gap nobody fixed: every advisor delivered a confident verdict despite the prompt withholding comp, equity, and financial runway — any one of which could flip this. That's not a reason to withhold an answer, but it's a reason to hold the answer as conditional on what's known.

### The Recommendation

Take the Senior IC offer — Option A.

The case for it doesn't rest on the council's shakiest reasoning (the Expansionist's 2026-market speculation, which the rest of the council didn't buy). It rests on the parts that survived scrutiny from every direction: a tested preference (coding over meetings) against an untested narrative ("should be strategic"); a false dichotomy, since Staff/Principal tracks deliver strategic scope without the role change; a reversibility asymmetry that favors staying technical now and managing later, not the reverse; and a real offer being compared against a track that doesn't concretely exist yet. Four of five advisors reached this outright; the fifth reached it conditionally — and the condition points the same direction, because if a senior-IC ladder existed at the current company, it would likely have been the offer, not management.

Honest caveat: this is the right call on what's been shared. It could flip if the smaller company turns out to be financially shaky, if the comp delta is large enough to matter, or if there's context about the current company's IC ladder that never made it into the prompt. That's not hedging the recommendation — it's naming the two or three facts that would actually change it.

### The One Thing to Do First

Before signing anything, have one direct conversation with current leadership this week: tell them you have a competing Senior IC offer, and ask point-blank whether a Staff or Principal IC track exists above your level. A real answer with specifics, in writing, changes the calculus. A stall or a no is itself the answer — and it means you take Option A.
