### Council Synthesis

**Areas of Convergence:**
- This isn't a binary "quit and enroll or don't" decision — it's an untested hypothesis, and the council converged on testing it before betting the job on it.
- "Genuinely curious about the research" as originally stated is too vague to evaluate. Both Dana and Priya explicitly conceded their own opening arguments leaned on that same vagueness.
- The concrete test the council landed on: a priced, time-boxed period of real research contact — shadowing a working researcher or a lab meeting in your specific field, or an unpaid/part-time RA stint — run *alongside* your current job, not after quitting it.
- What that test should measure isn't just whether you show up, but how the hours feel: absorption in the problem versus relief at being away from your day job.
- Field specificity matters and hasn't been supplied yet — placement rates, comp premiums, and whether a PhD helps at all vary enormously by field, both inside and outside academia.
- The test has a real cost (unpaid hours, forgone rest, a year of raises) and should be priced honestly rather than treated as free just because it's reversible.

**Remaining Disagreements:**
- What a passed test actually proves. Marcus maintains that even enthusiastic follow-through on a stacked RA test doesn't rule out identity repair or a status crisis — someone chasing the story will grind through it too — while Dana, Priya, and Sofia treat behavioral follow-through itself as strong enough signal.
- Sequencing weight: Dana still wants the test costed in hard numbers before and after, keeping financial analysis as a running check throughout; Sofia and Priya treat the money question as strictly downstream — worth doing only once the behavioral signal already looks real.
- How much the academic career question deserves to anchor the decision at all: Priya pushes hard that non-academic placement and comp data are the only numbers that matter for someone who's already said no to the professorship; Dana partially conceded this but hasn't fully dropped her broader skepticism that "curiosity" is a strong enough justification for the underlying six-figure decision.

**Recommended Path:**
There is a version of this where going back to school makes sense — but it's earned in stages, not assumed from the curiosity itself. First, name the actual field and get specific about what's pulling you: a particular problem, a method, the depth of unbroken time, or the identity of "researcher" — those point to different programs and different tests. Second, before changing your job status at all, run a priced, time-boxed period of real contact with that field — shadow a working researcher, sit in on lab meetings, or take on unpaid/part-time research work on top of your current job for a defined stretch (six to eight weeks is enough to surface a pattern). Third, judge that period on how it felt from the inside, not just whether you completed it: absorption in the problem is a different signal than relief at escaping your day job, and only you can tell those apart. Fourth, only if the desire survives that contact test unshaken, do the full financial reckoning — actual program, actual funding package, actual field-specific placement and comp data, actual cost of the test itself layered on top of the degree's cost. If it clears all four checkpoints, you're not romanticizing it, you're underwriting it with evidence. If it collapses at any of them, that's not a failure of nerve — that's the answer, arrived at for a lot less than a resignation letter.
