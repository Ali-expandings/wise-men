## Decision / Answer
Seven independent reasoning paths — precedent, base rates, incentives, falsification, first principles — converged on the same structural move even though none of them saw each other's work: "is there a version where this makes sense" can't be answered from where you're standing now, because you're missing the two facts that would decide it. Don't quit and enroll on current information. Don't decide not to, either. Spend roughly the next 1–3 months finding out:

1. **Question or vocation?** Is this a bounded thing you want to understand — after which you'd happily go back to other work — or how you'd actually want to spend research-shaped decades? If you can name the specific research role you'd want at 50 and confirm with people already in it that a PhD is genuinely how they got there, that's a vocation case. If you can't name it, you'd be buying an experience, not a career — which can still be worth it, but as a knowingly-priced purchase, not something you back into.
2. **Tested or untested?** How long has this pull existed? If it predates your current job by years and survived earlier busy periods, that's real signal. If it showed up around when the job started feeling stale, you may be diagnosing career dissatisfaction as intellectual curiosity — a PhD is an expensive way to find that out.
3. **Reachable without enrolling, or not?** Email 2–3 labs or researchers in the field about part-time RA work, auditing, or co-authoring one real piece of output (a preprint, a replication, a workshop paper) in the next 9–12 months. Whether anyone says yes is itself the test: in most fields you can get real research contact without enrolling; in a handful (lab sciences especially) you genuinely can't, and that changes everything below.
4. **Does your field/country have a structural bridge?** Industrial PhDs, Engineering Doctorates, DBA/EdD-style practitioner tracks, or a negotiated part-time/leave arrangement with your current employer can let you keep income while doing the work. If one exists and fits, it likely dissolves this whole binary.
5. **What do your specific numbers say?** Talk to 5–10 people who did a PhD in your subfield and did *not* stay in academia. What did the degree actually do for their current work? What was the real stipend-vs-salary gap and time-to-completion, not the reference-class estimate? Would they do it again at your age and income?

If you have a partner or anyone else financially entangled with your income, do this before any of the above, not after: have the actual-numbers conversation — years, income drop, savings drawdown — and weight their unscripted reaction over their first polite answer. This was the single most distinctly-argued point in the entire council, and the question as you've asked it doesn't mention it at all.

Only revisit "quit and enroll" once you have answers to 1–5. If they converge — real vocation, tested pull, a named field-specific reason the credential matters, funded or bridged — the council's answer is yes. If they don't converge, the honest read of your own question is that you're pricing the story of having left a good job for research higher than the research itself, which is the "romanticizing" you asked about.

**Cost of being wrong**: this recommendation is cheap to be wrong about. The test costs months, not years, and doesn't foreclose enrolling — most application cycles still work on a 9–12 month test's timeline. The one scenario where the caution itself costs something real: if your field is one where genuine research contact truly requires enrollment (Devil's Advocate's own named weak spot), the "prove it first, outside the program" test isn't available, and a full year of testing delays a genuine vocation case by a full year in a field where starting younger matters. If that's your situation, weight Long-term Self's and Historian's more permissive read higher than the default above.
