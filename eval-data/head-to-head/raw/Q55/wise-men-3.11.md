## Recommendation

There is a real version of this that makes sense, but it is not the version the question poses (quit now, enroll full-time, stay unsure about academia). The version worth pursuing is: prove the curiosity is load-bearing while still employed, then move only into a structure that does not require burning the well-paying job on a guess — a funded or salaried offer, a part-time or company-sponsored program, or a research role that pays you to do the work. Resignation, if it happens at all, comes last, after the evidence is in hand.

## Why

The stakes are asymmetric: staying employed while testing costs little and stays reversible; resigning into an unfunded or exploratory full-time program does not (walking back into an equivalent role and salary years later is not guaranteed, and the years and retirement growth are gone regardless of outcome — unverified, check actual comp trajectory and retirement account numbers against a financial planner).

"Curious about the research but unsure about the academic career" doesn't need to be resolved by the PhD itself — a PhD is worth doing for reasons other than wanting a professorship (industry or applied research roles, or simply as a chosen, self-funded pursuit), but only if one of those reasons is actually true for you, specifically, not in the abstract.

The honest test for "romanticizing" isn't introspection, it's evidence you can gather this month: whether you've already produced any unpaid, self-directed output on this question in the last year (a draft, an analysis, an email to a researcher — not just reading and podcasts), and whether real job postings for the non-academic role you picture actually require or reward a PhD. Absence of both is the strongest single signal that the pull is about escaping the current job rather than about the work itself. Caveat: a demanding job can suppress this kind of output for years without disproving real interest — treat this as evidence, not proof, in either direction.

The common "audit a class, talk to a professor" version of testing has a specific failure mode: it shows the appealing parts of research, not the grind, and it's easy to mistake enjoying ideas in a field for tolerating the actual work of producing them. A stronger test is doing something closer to the real thing — a small project, a redone analysis, a trial collaboration — sustained over months, not a single conversation or a one-off course.

Program structure changes the math more than age does. Time-to-degree and completion odds vary a great deal by country and field (UK programs are commonly shorter, around 3-4 years; US programs commonly run longer, often cited around 5-7 years; some European PhDs are salaried positions rather than stipended study), and by whether the offer is genuinely fully funded for its real, not nominal, length. These specifics are unverified — check the specific program's funding letter, its own completion/placement data, and current national norms for the country and field in question — and should be confirmed before any decision, not assumed from general reputation.

The decision doesn't only affect the person deciding: if anyone else's financial or day-to-day stability is tied to the current income, the choice functions differently depending on whether that has been discussed and bounded in advance versus assumed.

## What to do

1. **This week** (no cost): write down the actual research question, not just the topic, and write down what job you would want at 40-42 under three scenarios — academic, industry/applied research, or back in your current field. If none of the three genuinely requires the PhD, be honest that you'd be paying for the experience itself — still a legitimate choice if you can afford it outright, but it changes the bar.
2. **Next 2-4 weeks** (a handful of evenings, no cost): pull 15-20 current job postings for the non-academic role you picture and count how many actually require or reward a PhD versus equivalent experience. Ask 2-3 people already doing that job whether they'd hire a new PhD over someone with six more years of relevant experience — a sharper test than the postings alone. Separately, total up what you've personally produced, not read or watched, on this topic in the last 12 months.
3. **Same window**: talk to 3-5 people currently a few years into a similar program (ideally people who weren't chasing academia either), and 1-2 people who left a PhD partway. If there's a partner or anyone financially entangled with you, have the direct conversation about a bounded version of this — a time box, an income floor, a checkpoint — rather than an open-ended sacrifice.
4. **From roughly month 2 through month 6-8, while still employed**: run something closer to the real work than a class or a conversation — a small project with a lab or research group, a redone analysis you publish yourself, a trial collaboration. The test is whether it still pulls you after the boring parts and the dead ends, not whether the idea of it does.
5. **Before applying**: only target fully funded, salaried, or part-time/employer-linked programs (structures like industry-sponsored or company-salaried doctorates exist in some fields and countries — confirm what applies to yours). Get funding terms in writing: how many guaranteed years, whether they cover the program's real length, what happens if a grant or assistantship falls through, whether the stipend is realistic against local rent, and whether your target country requires a separate master's first.
6. **If an offer arrives**: compute the real cost — net income lost × real program length, plus foregone retirement growth and seniority — against your savings and anyone who depends on you, and set a fixed checkpoint (commonly year 1 or 2) to reassess. Resign only once the offer is signed, not before, and not on the strength of the application alone.

## Risks of this plan

Running the multi-month trial costs time and won't feel as decisive as just deciding — the risk of waiting is a few months of delay against career trajectory and, if you're financially comfortable and the pull is real, some anxiety of sitting with an open question longer.

The trial itself can mislead in both directions: a good trial experience under low stakes and a supportive setting doesn't fully replicate a real program with a real advisor's incentives attached years in, and a bad few weeks doesn't fully rule it out either — treat the trial as evidence, not a verdict.

If the trial is skipped and the move is straight to resigning into a full-time program, the more likely failure mode isn't "you hate research" — it's sunk-cost drift: two or three years in, without an academic-career goal to justify it, there's real pressure to make the sacrifice "count" by chasing the very academic path you said you didn't want, or to grind out a degree that no longer serves the reason you started.

If a partner, family, or dependent's stability rides on your income and that hasn't been made explicit and bounded in advance, the strain commonly surfaces later, not immediately — once "temporary" quietly becomes the new normal.

Cost of being wrong: this plan is close to fully reversible if followed as written — a trial costs months, not years, and nothing here requires resigning before you have hard evidence and, ideally, a signed offer in hand. The one place it stops being reversible is the moment you actually resign; everything upstream of that point is designed to be walked back cheaply if the evidence doesn't hold up.

## Strongest counter-position

The case against testing your way into this at all: the question "is there any version where this makes sense" is close to unfalsifiable — phrased that way, it will always find a yes, because "the right conditions" can absorb any counterexample. The stronger frame is that a PhD, past the first year or two, is mostly a job, not school — years of work under one advisor's incentives, on a narrow question, at a fraction of current pay, inside a system still built mainly to produce academics you've said you don't want to become. Being "curious about the research but unsure about the career it leads to" is the same shape as going to law school without wanting to practice law: wanting the process while being unsure about the one thing the credential actually buys. On that view, the real move isn't to test your way toward quitting — it's to treat this as a job problem and solve it as one: a research-heavy role in industry, government, or a nonprofit; a negotiated reduced schedule or leave that keeps income while collaborating with a research group; or, only if a specific job genuinely requires the credential, a part-time or salaried/company-sponsored doctorate that never requires full resignation in the first place. This position would be wrong if real, unpaid research work has already been done and hit a wall only an institution can remove — a lab, equipment, restricted data, ethics approval — or if a job worth wanting regardless of academia verifiably lists the PhD as a requirement. Both are worth checking before dismissing it.

## Confidence

High: "unsure about an academic career" does not disqualify a PhD from making sense, provided the reason for doing it is something other than the professorship, and that reason is checked against real job postings and real funding terms rather than assumed.

Medium: the specific completion rates, typical time-to-degree, and academic-job-market placement figures referenced above are plausible and internally consistent but unverified — check the specific program's own completion and placement data, and current national/field norms, before relying on any of them.

Medium: the "did you already produce unpaid output on this" test is a real signal but not proof either way — a demanding job can suppress it for years without meaning the interest isn't genuine, so treat a "no" here as a prompt to investigate, not a verdict.
