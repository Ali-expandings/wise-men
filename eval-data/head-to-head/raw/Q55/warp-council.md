## Recommendation

There's a real, non-romanticized version of this — but it isn't "quit and enroll in a 5-year PhD," it's "spend the next couple of months finding out, cheaply and reversibly, whether the specific thing you're curious about survives contact with the boring parts of research, before you touch your job." All three council members converged on this sequencing independently, despite disagreeing on how likely the PhD itself is to be the right end point.

## Why

- **"Curious about the research" is at least three different feelings, and only one of them justifies a PhD.** Curiosity about specific open problems you want to personally spend years closing is a PhD-shaped desire. Curiosity about a field as a reader, attraction to the identity of "researcher," or dissatisfaction with the current job wearing a curiosity costume are not — and a PhD serves those badly or not at all. The council's sharpest disagreement is really just a different prior on which of these is true for you, which you're the only one who can actually resolve — reflection alone won't do it, but doing something research-shaped for a few months will.
- **The line that should give you pause is the one you wrote yourself: "not sure I want an academic career."** A PhD is institutionally built to produce academics — advisor, thesis committee, the whole apparatus — so wanting the degree without wanting what it's for is worth being suspicious of. That said, it isn't disqualifying: it stops being a red flag if your field pays PhDs to do research outside academia, if the project builds on expertise your career already gave you rather than resetting you to zero, or if you consciously decide to buy the experience for its own sake and can afford to.
- **"Leave a well-paying job" undersells the real cost and overstates the binary.** The honest cost isn't just the stipend-vs-salary gap for 4-6 years — it's that gap plus lost compounding in what are often prime retirement-saving years, plus a career-seniority reset around age 40-41. That can easily run into several hundred thousand dollars in foregone earnings and growth depending on field and country. But "quit or don't" is a false binary: a research master's (1-2 years, much lower stakes, often convertible), industry-sponsored or company-partnered PhDs (CIFRE-style in France, Industrial PhD in Denmark, CASE in the UK), part-time or executive doctorates, and research-adjacent industry roles are all real, commonly-used structures that don't require walking away from income. How many apply depends on your field and country — worth an afternoon of research before assuming full resignation is the only door.
- **The decision is cheaply testable before it's irreversible.** Talk to five to ten people who've actually done this — some who finished, some who quit, some who left academia afterward — and ask about the ordinary week, not the highlights. Spend real time on the actual texture of the work: read the papers, run a trial research project, audit a course, informally collaborate with a lab. See if the pull survives the slow, stuck, unglamorous parts. If it does, and a specific problem — not just a field — is what's pulling you, that's a materially different signal than "I'd like my life to be about ideas" surviving only in the abstract.

## Tradeoffs and risks

- **Testing too long:** "test first" can quietly become permission to never decide. Attach a hard deadline to the testing window — run it alongside an actual application cycle, not instead of one — or staying becomes the default by inertia rather than by choice.
- **Testing too little in resource-heavy fields:** if your field needs lab equipment or institutional access to do anything research-shaped, side-project testing is harder to run, and the absence of a side project means less than it would in a computational or theoretical field. Don't over-read a negative result if your field structurally prevents a good test.
- **Solving the wrong problem:** if what's actually driving this is dissatisfaction with the current job rather than a pull toward research specifically, a PhD is an expensive way to solve a problem a new job or role change would fix faster and more reversibly. One useful check: would the pull survive the job getting genuinely better? If not, treat the job as the actual problem.
- **Committing without funding or fit:** if you do move toward a traditional PhD, an unfunded offer or a mediocre advisor match is a bigger risk factor than the degree itself — the advisor shapes the day-to-day experience more than almost anything else.

## Final call

Do now: over the next couple of months, without touching your job — (1) talk to several people who've actually lived this, including some who left or regretted it; (2) do something genuinely research-shaped in the specific area you think you're curious about and see if it holds up past the interesting parts; (3) find out concretely which non-resignation structures (research master's, sponsored or industrial PhD, part-time route, research-adjacent industry role) actually exist in your field and country. Set a real decision date at the end of that window.

Optional future hardening, only if the tests hold up: apply only to funded programs, weight advisor fit and program structure as heavily as the topic itself, and if you do commit, write your own exit criteria down before you start — the size of the sacrifice tends to make people grind through a program that's stopped serving them, so it's easier to set the bar while you're still clear-headed than after you've already given something up for it.
