## Recommendation

Don't choose between per-seat and per-usage — both break under a 10x usage spread, just for different customers. Move to a hybrid: an annual plan fee (upfront where you can get it) that bundles an included usage allowance, metered overage above that allowance priced well below what a light customer effectively pays per unit today, and negotiated volume commitments for your largest accounts. Bill in a unit customers can see and that tracks what they actually get from the API — successful transactions or active end-users, not raw calls inflated by retries and polling. Before you build or announce anything, spend one to two weeks confirming this is solving a real problem: pull the last 6-12 months of usage and billing data and check whether your heaviest accounts actually cost more to serve than they pay today, and whether any deals or renewals have already been lost or strained over the current pricing. If that data doesn't show real harm, the safe move is to launch the new pricing to new customers only and leave existing accounts alone.

## Why

A seat is a proxy for a human clicking through a dashboard; on an API, machines generate the traffic, one API key can carry an entire company's load, and seat count tracks headcount, not usage or cost. With a 10x usage spread, flat per-seat pricing means your lightest customers subsidize your heaviest ones, and if serving each call costs you real money, your biggest accounts can be your least profitable. Pure per-usage pricing fixes that mismatch but creates two new problems: customers can't predict or budget their bills, and your own revenue becomes harder to forecast and more exposed to customers optimizing their usage down. A hybrid — a fee that buys predictability plus overage that captures the tail — is the structural answer to a spread this size, and it's also the pattern usage-heavy API and infrastructure vendors converge on once they have enough customer diversity to need it (unverified in the specifics — Twilio, AWS and similar vendors are usage-based from the start, while enterprise usage deals in this space commonly layer commitments on top for predictability).

The 10x figure itself is worth a second look before it drives the decision: that's a narrower spread than many API businesses see between their smallest and largest accounts (unverified), and nothing in the situation as described shows the current model is actually failing — no lost deals, no margin loss, no churn cited. It's entirely possible the fix here is three or four usage-banded tiers differentiated by throughput, support and SLA, with no metered billing at all, rather than a full move to metered overage. Which of these you need depends on one fact you can check directly: whether each unit of usage costs you real money to serve. If it does, metering is justified. If it doesn't — if your heaviest accounts are heavy because of inefficient integrations (polling instead of webhooks, no caching, retry storms) rather than because they extract 10x the value — then billing by raw usage mostly penalizes bad engineering on both sides, and a simpler tiered structure serves you better.

The revolt risk is asymmetric in a way that's easy to underprice: in any repricing, cuts for the customers who end up paying less take effect immediately and in full, while increases for the customers who end up paying more get negotiated down, engineered away through usage optimization, or lost entirely to churn. A price list that looks revenue-neutral on paper routinely loses money in year one for exactly this reason, and the accounts most likely to push back — your heaviest, most integrated users — are usually also the accounts you can least afford to lose. Revolts also tend to trace back to how a change lands rather than to the pricing logic itself: retroactive changes, opaque billing units customers can't verify against their own logs, and short notice are the recurring pattern behind the worst-known pricing backlashes in this space (unverified in the specifics — both a mobile-engine vendor's 2023 per-install fee and a developer-tool vendor's 2025 shift to usage-based limits drew public backlash largely because customers couldn't see or predict the number they were being billed on).

## What to do

First, verify before you build: pull 6-12 months of real usage and billing data per account and check (a) whether your top 10% of accounts by usage actually cost more to serve than they pay, (b) whether heavy usage is stable month to month or inflated by retries/polling/inefficient integration, (c) whether usage correlates with account size, renewal and expansion, and (d) any contract terms — price locks, notice requirements, most-favored-customer clauses — that constrain how you can change existing accounts. Budget one analyst for one to two weeks. If this data shows no real margin or retention problem, stop here and launch the new model to new customers only.

If it does show a problem, design the hybrid — plan tiers, included allowance, overage rate, and unit of measure — and re-bill every existing account's real usage under two or three candidate structures before telling anyone. Tune the tiers until the accounts facing real increases are few enough to handle one by one and the projected first-year revenue loss (cuts land in full; increases only partially stick — assume roughly 70% of a given increase actually sticks as a planning baseline) is one your usage growth can recover within 12-18 months. Build usage tracking, a customer-facing dashboard, spend alerts and a spend cap before anyone sees a usage-based invoice — trust in the billing math matters as much as the price level.

Then roll out in gated phases: new customers only for a full quarter or two, watching conversion and support tickets against your recent baseline; then, for existing customers, send two or three cycles of preview invoices ("this is what you would have paid") before any money moves, with account managers walking your largest at-risk accounts through their numbers directly rather than a mass email; then migrate at renewal only, with at least 90 days' notice, a cap on how much any single renewal can increase, and volume-discounted annual commitments offered to your heaviest accounts. Let light accounts opt into savings immediately. Set stop conditions before you start — cancellations beating your modeled forecast, support tickets shifting from confusion to disputes, or heavy accounts' traffic silently dropping while they stay nominally active are all signals to pause the next wave rather than push through it.

## Risks of this plan

Modeling takes real time and heavy accounts may still push back even with warning, notice and phased increases — this plan trades speed for lower revolt risk, and if your cost-to-serve problem is urgent (accounts actively losing you money today), every week of diagnostic work is a week of continued losses. Conversely, the diagnostic phase can also save you from a costly, disruptive migration that turns out to address a problem you don't actually have: metering, dashboards and billing infrastructure are expensive to build and hard to unwind once customers are used to seeing a usage number on their invoice. The plan's own numbers show that even a well-designed switch can lose money in year one if increases don't stick at the rate assumed — treat any specific stick-rate or payback-period figure you calculate as a planning assumption to pressure-test, not a guarantee. If your metering isn't accurate enough for customers to reconcile against their own logs, the trust cost of one disputed invoice will outweigh anything gained on pricing precision. Finally, moving revenue from upfront seat payments to any usage-billed-in-arrears component delays cash and typically increases invoice disputes, even when total revenue is unchanged.

If you get this wrong, it's recoverable but not cheap: a botched migration costs you the specific accounts that revolted (usually your largest and most reference-able), the engineering and support time spent building and running metering, and some trust that takes longer to rebuild than to lose. The gated, renewal-anchored rollout above is designed specifically so a bad signal shows up while only a small slice of customers has been touched, not after you've moved everyone.

## Strongest counter-position

The case for not building metered overage at all, and for not assuming existing customers need to move: seat-vs-usage is being treated here as a pricing-mechanism question, but nothing in the situation demonstrates it's actually broken yet — no cited margin loss, no churn, no lost deals, just the observation that usage varies. A 10x spread between lightest and heaviest customer is, on its own, a narrow range for an API business (unverified) — narrow enough that three or four tiers differentiated by usage allowance, throughput, support tier and SLA can cover the whole base without a single customer ever seeing a per-call charge on an invoice. Going further and billing raw usage risks charging customers for integration inefficiency (retries, polling, no caching) rather than for value delivered, which means the "fix" mostly penalizes bad engineering and evaporates as soon as heavy accounts clean up their integrations — the revenue gain a metered model promises on paper is not the revenue gain it delivers in practice. And because cuts for winners land in full while increases for losers get fought, cached-around, or walked away from, a forced migration can spend real engineering and trust budget while capturing little of the modeled upside. This view wins if the verification step above shows usage doesn't track real cost or value, or shows no current harm at all — in which case the right move is to ship simple bands, hold existing customers on their current terms, and revisit only if the data changes.

## Confidence

If that assumption is wrong, a simpler tiered structure without metering is likely better, not just safer.

High that the phased, renewal-gated rollout (model first, new customers only, preview invoices, capped increases at renewal, direct outreach to top accounts, named stop conditions) reduces revolt risk relative to any abrupt or blanket switch — this is standard risk-mitigation logic that doesn't depend on any single unverified fact, and it degrades gracefully even if some specific number in it (a stick rate, a payback period) turns out to be off.

Low on whether you need to migrate existing customers at all right now — that depends entirely on data not stated in the question: your actual cost per unit of usage, your current margin on heavy accounts, and whether any deal or renewal has already been lost or strained over pricing. This is the single most important thing to establish before committing further time to this.

Material unknowns, in order of how much they'd change the plan: whether usage tracks real cost/value or mostly reflects integration patterns (named above); your real cost-to-serve curve and current margin by account size; whether your 10x figure reflects stable differences between customers (which points toward differentiated tiers) or the same customers' own usage swinging month to month (which points toward commitments and banded smoothing instead); and any contractual constraints on how existing accounts can be repriced.
