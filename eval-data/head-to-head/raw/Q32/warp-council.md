## Recommendation

Write as a calm, specific peer, not a supplicant or a salesman: one sharp, verifiable observation about the VP's business, one concrete proof point about you, one small and easy-to-decline ask, and a neutral sign-off. Then — more important than any of that — make sure the email couldn't be sent to 50 other VPs unchanged. All three council members converged on the same shape; the swing factor isn't tone, it's specificity.

## Why

- **Desperate and arrogant are the same bug wearing different clothes.** Every move that reads as desperate (apologizing for existing, hedging, flattery, thanks-in-advance, an open-ended ask) and every move that reads as arrogant (presumptive next steps, teaching them their own business, vague "let's explore synergies," unearned peer framing) both make the email about you — your need, or your standing — instead of about them. Fix the centering problem and both failure modes disappear together; there's no "middle" tone to hunt for.
- **The lever most drafts miss is relevance, not register.** The contrarian review's strongest point: a well-toned email that could be sent to 50 other VPs unchanged still gets ignored. One specific, checkable observation about their business — something only someone who actually looked would know — does more work than any amount of tone-polishing. Register is what's left over once the email is genuinely about them.
- **The ask is usually miscalibrated in both directions.** Desperate drafts leave the ask vague and open-ended ("would love to grab your brain sometime") — costly for the reader to answer. Arrogant drafts leave the ask presumptive ("I'll send a Thursday invite") — costly for the reader to decline gracefully. The fix in both cases is the same: one small, binary, easy-to-decline ask, not a bigger or smaller one.
- **"Partner" is doing too much work.** All three reports flagged this independently: an undefined partnership ask reads as evasive at best and a disguised pitch at worst. Naming the concrete shape (integration, referral, co-marketing, pilot, channel) removes a whole axis of suspicion before register even enters the picture.

## Tradeoffs and risks

- **Context this advice can't see:** whether any warm path exists (even a weak mutual connection) — if one does, use it; it outweighs any register fix. Also unknown: industry/region (relationship-first cultures and markets outside the US/Europe expect a warmer opening line than the peer-brevity default here), company size (enterprise VPs are more likely to forward to an assistant or ignore outright, so the email needs to stand alone with zero context), and what specifically read wrong in the three existing drafts — worth checking those against the list below rather than rewriting from zero.
- **Overcorrection risk:** trimming toward terse peer-brevity can itself read as cold in relationship-first contexts. This is a default for cold, transactional B2B outreach, not a universal register.
- **Reply rates stay low regardless of craft.** Two to three spaced follow-ups, each adding new information (never "just bumping this"), matter about as much as the first email's wording.

## Final call

Rewrite draft four against this checklist: subject line 3–6 words, no pitch in it; body under ~150 words; first line a specific, verifiable fact about them, not about you; exactly one concrete proof point (a name, a number, a shipped result — no adjectives about your own work); exactly one small, binary ask with a named concrete shape for "partner"; a line that makes it easy to decline or redirect; neutral sign-off, no thanks-in-advance. If a fourth draft still feels off, the likelier cause per this council is genericity, not tone — check whether the email would survive having the company's name swapped for a competitor's.
