## Why

Blameless is a rule about how the document treats people: no punishment for an honest, reported action, because punishing honesty is what makes the next near-miss invisible until it's worse. Accountability is a rule about what the organization owes afterward: naming who owns each fix, whether it actually ships, and stating the customer impact plainly. These aren't opposing pressures on the same sentence — they apply to different parts of the document. Concretely: narrate actions and decisions in neutral, causal language, including what the person knew and why their call looked reasonable at the time (never "should have," "failed to," "neglected," or a single "root cause" pinned to a person) — and give every remediation item a named owner, a date, and a way to check it actually happened. Passive voice around a fact the team already knows ("the alert was silenced") does not buy safety — it reads as cover-up to the person who knows exactly whose hand did it, and vagueness in the fix list reads as a team that doesn't have a grip on its own system. Precise about the mechanism, silent about assigning character — both at once, same document.

There's also a real edge the clean "blame the system, not the person" framing doesn't cover on its own: whoever decides what counts as "the system" is making a judgment call, and that call quietly protects whoever's decision gets absorbed into passive systemic language — most often someone senior to the person writing the document. "Remediation for the known failover bug had not been scheduled" and "Failover fix deferred in planning for Feature Y; decision owner: Eng Director" describe the same fact; only one of them is accountable. Finally, if any version of this document reaches customers, don't write one document for both jobs. ## What to do

1. Before drafting anything (15–30 minutes): ask your manager directly what "accountability" means to them here — ownership of fixes with follow-through, or a name attached to consequence in the document — and whether any performance or disciplinary process already touches anyone involved. If one does, you cannot honestly offer that person a no-blame conversation, and that changes how you gather information in step 2. Propose the working definition: every contributing factor, including a management or prioritization decision if one is in the chain, gets an owning role and a dated fix; anything about a specific person's conduct goes to a private conversation, never the shared document. Get your manager to accept or reject that definition explicitly — don't infer it from tone.
2. Build the factual timeline from logs, tickets, and chat history, then close the gaps with short private 1:1s (30–45 minutes each) with the 2–5 people closest to the incident, starting with whoever was nearest the trigger. Ask what they expected, what they saw, why the action seemed reasonable at the time, and whether others would routinely do the same thing under the same pressure — that last question is what turns "one person's mistake" into a systemic factor, and it's also the raw material for the test in step 5.
3. Draft the narrative: the subject of every sentence about what happened is the action or the system, never a person's character; include what the person knew and why the call was reasonable at the time; no "should have," "failed to," or a single named root cause.
4. Draft remediation as a table: item, owner (a name, not a team), date, verification step. This is where accountability actually lives in the document — an item with no owner or date is the most common way a "blameless" postmortem quietly becomes toothless.
5. Apply one test per decision point in the timeline: would a similarly-positioned peer, with the same information and pressure, plausibly have done the same thing? If yes, it's an honest-error, system-fix case. It fails — and needs a different, private conversation, not document language — only if someone knowingly bypassed a rule they knew existed, concealed what happened, or repeated an identical failure after an explicit prior correction, or if a decision above your level materially contributed (flag it, don't resolve it alone).
6. If this goes to customers: write the internal version first and completely; then write a separate, shorter customer note (impact, plain-language cause, fixes with dates, no individuals) — don't redact the internal draft.
7. Have whoever's actions are most closely described read that section before wider circulation, and let them choose how their part is framed; on a small team a role is not real anonymity.
8. Have your manager pre-read before the team review specifically for where the two asks collide — usually a "why didn't they just follow the process" reaction to a systemic-gap sentence — and resolve it by strengthening the fix, not by adding blame language back in.
9. Check with legal/comms if the outage involves data exposure, an SLA, or contractual notice obligations before anything customer-facing goes out.

## Risks of this plan

Undercorrecting: vague, passive language chosen to "protect" someone reads as evasive to leadership and as cover-up to the people who already know what happened — it satisfies neither ask and erodes trust with both audiences at once. Overcorrecting: treating every contributing decision as fair game for named specificity, including a colleague's or superior's reasonable-at-the-time call, re-creates exactly the blame culture that makes the next incident get hidden instead of reported. Accountability theater is the quiet failure mode either way: remediation items with no owner, no date, and no verification step look strong on the page and change nothing, and the same root cause recurs. The asymmetric case is the one most likely to bite you specifically: a junior action gets described in granular, honest detail while a senior deferral or overruled objection gets softened into passive systemic language — that pattern is visible to anyone who was in the room, and it costs you credibility with your own team even if it satisfies your manager.

Cost of being wrong: this is not irreversible in the sense of a legal filing, but a postmortem that reads as either a cover-up or a scapegoat hunt is hard to walk back once circulated — it's a document people remember, and it shapes whether the next incident gets self-reported honestly or hidden until it's worse. If you get the wording right but the manager's actual ask was for a named consequence, you'll have to renegotiate scope after the fact — a rewrite and an awkward conversation, cheaper than guessing wrong the other way and having the document damage someone's standing or your team's psychological safety on the way to press.
