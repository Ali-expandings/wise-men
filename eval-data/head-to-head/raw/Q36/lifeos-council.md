## Council Debate: Writing a Blameless Postmortem That Also Delivers Accountability

**Council Members:**
- **Jordan** (incident commander / SRE lead — writes postmortems for a living, insists blameless and accountable are the same doc in different sections)
- **Reese** (skeptic, ex-engineering-manager — has seen "blameless" used to launder real violations, pushes for naming decisions honestly)
- **Priya** (pragmatic engineering manager — focused on audience, incentives, and psychological safety)
- **Sam** (analyst — grounds the debate in the Just Culture framework and industry precedent: Allspaw/Etsy, Google SRE)

### Round 1: Initial Positions

**Jordan (incident commander / SRE lead):**
Blameless and accountable are the same document, different sections, and if you're conflating them you'll write a mush that satisfies neither your manager nor the next incident. Blameless governs the narrative: the timeline. Facts only — what happened, when, who did what action, no adjectives, no "should have." "Engineer deployed config X at 14:32" not "engineer carelessly pushed unreviewed config." If a human made a judgment call that looked reasonable at the time with the information they had, that's not the root cause — that's a symptom. Push past it: why did the information they had look sufficient? Why did the system let that judgment call reach prod without a guardrail? That's your contributing-factors section, and it's where 90% of the postmortem's weight should sit. Accountability governs the action items table: named owner, not a team name, hard date, and a follow-up mechanism that isn't "we'll remember." Every action item traces back to a specific contributing factor in the timeline. If you can't name who owns the fix and by when, you don't have accountability — you have a wish list.

**Reese (skeptic, ex-engineering-manager):**
Blameless doesn't mean consequence-free — I've watched that conflation kill three good postmortem cultures. Here's the distinction: blameless protects people from punishment for surfacing the truth. It does not protect decisions from scrutiny. Write it like this: name the specific action in neutral, factual language — "the on-call engineer merged the config change without the required second review" — not "John screwed up," but also not laundering it into "a review gap was identified" when a review process existed and someone routed around it. If your team has a code-review requirement and someone skipped it, that's not a systemic gap, that's a violated control. Say so. Then the accountability lives in the action items: was this a one-off lapse, or a pattern? Does it need a process fix (harder to skip), a training conversation, or — if it's willful and repeated — a management conversation outside the doc? The postmortem's job is accurate causation. It's not obligated to pretend every root cause is systemic when it isn't.

**Priya (pragmatic engineering manager):**
Blameless doesn't mean nobody's accountable — it means the failure mode you're accountable for is the system, not the person who happened to be holding the pager. First thing I'd nail down with your manager: when they say "accountability," do they mean the customer-facing commitment — we own this, here's the fix, here's the date — or internal pressure to name who screwed up? Those produce completely different documents. Practically, split it by audience. Customer-facing summary: what broke, impact, what you're doing, timeline — ownership language, zero blame, zero internal jargon. Internal engineering doc: full timeline, contributing factors framed as "the system allowed X" not "Bob did X," and a contributing-factors section with owners and dates for follow-through — that's your accountability, tracked as commitments, not confessions. If people read the postmortem and feel exposed, they'll sandbag the next one, and you'll get a worse outage from a smaller warning sign nobody flagged.

**Sam (analyst — Just Culture / Allspaw / Google SRE):**
Blameless and accountable aren't opposites—they answer different questions. Blameless asks "why did this make sense to the person at the time?" Accountable asks "who owns fixing it?" You keep them separate by structure, not tone. Write a factual timeline first: what happened, when, what the on-call saw. Then run a five-whys or contributing-factors analysis — this section stays blameless because you're asking "what in the system let this happen," not "who screwed up." Someone deployed without checking a dashboard? Ask why that was possible, why the dashboard wasn't part of the checklist, why the alert didn't fire earlier. Then, separately: corrective actions. Named owner, deadline, tracked to closure. That's your accountability — accountability for the fix, not punishment for the mistake. The only time you name an individual for consequences is if they knowingly bypassed a known safeguard — that's a reckless-violation case, not a normal incident, and it doesn't belong in this document at all.

Just Culture (Dekker, Marx) draws exactly this line: honest error and at-risk behavior get systemic analysis; reckless or willful violation of a known safeguard gets individual accountability, often outside the postmortem. You're not disagreeing with me, you're specifying the boundary condition I gestured at — routing around a required review, knowingly, is the reckless case. Say so explicitly and you've named it, not blamed it. Priya's audience split matches actual practice — Google's SRE postmortems and Etsy's blameless model both separate a short customer-facing summary from the internal technical doc with full contributing-factors analysis. That's not a new idea, it's the industry default, and it solves Jordan's "mush" problem structurally rather than tonally.

### Council Synthesis

**Areas of Convergence:**
- "Blameless" and "accountable" answer different questions, not opposite ones — blameless is an investigative stance (assume good faith, dig for systemic causes); accountable is the outcome (owned fixes, deadlines, and, rarely, individual consequences).
- Structure carries the distinction, not euphemism: a factual timeline naming actions precisely (not people's character), a contributing-factors section doing the systemic analysis (~90% of the document's weight), and a separate corrective-actions section with named owners and dates as the actual accountability mechanism.
- The Just Culture line (Dekker/Marx, echoed in Allspaw/Etsy and Google SRE practice) is the operative boundary: honest error and at-risk behavior get systemic treatment; reckless or willful violation of a known safeguard gets named individually — and that conversation happens outside the postmortem, not buried in it.
- Split by audience: a short, systemic, ownership-language summary for customers/leadership; a full, factually specific internal engineering doc for the team.
- Don't launder specific facts into vague systemic language ("a review gap was identified") when a control genuinely existed and was bypassed — that erodes trust in the document more than naming the fact would.

**Remaining Disagreements:**
- Where exactly to draw the line between "at-risk behavior" (systemic) and "reckless violation" (individual) — the council agrees this is a judgment call made against evidence case-by-case, not something a formula settles in advance. Reese's standing concern: teams will default to the comfortable systemic framing regardless of the facts unless someone actively resists that pull.

**Recommended Path:**
Write one internal postmortem with three structural pieces: (1) a strictly factual timeline that names specific actions in neutral language ("required second review was skipped" — not softened, not character-judged), (2) a contributing-factors analysis that stays systemic and carries most of the document's weight, asking why the system allowed the action to reach production, and (3) a corrective-actions section — named owner, hard deadline, tracked to closure — which is where the "accountability" your manager wants actually lives. Pair this with a short, systemic, blame-free summary for customers/leadership. If the evidence shows a genuine reckless or repeated willful violation of a known safeguard, that gets named as fact in the timeline but its consequence is handled in a management conversation outside the postmortem — the document's job is accurate causation and forward-looking fixes, not verdicts. Under this structure, "blameless but accountable" stops being a contradiction and becomes the actual design: blameless in tone and investigation, accountable in structure and follow-through.
