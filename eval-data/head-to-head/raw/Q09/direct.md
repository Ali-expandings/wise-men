Event sourcing tutorials show you a shopping cart with three event types and a happy-path replay function. Real systems have hundreds of event types evolving over years, and that's where it breaks. The actual failure modes, in the order they usually bite:

**Event schema evolution has no clean answer.** Your `OrderPlaced` event from 2023 has a different shape than the one you'd write today — a field got renamed, a new required field appeared, a bug produced malformed events for three weeks. Every consumer that replays history now has to handle every version that ever existed, forever. Teams underestimate this at design time because early on there's only one version. Two years in, you're writing upcasters/version-translation layers for events nobody remembers the original intent of. This is usually the first real pain point, and it's structural — it doesn't go away with better tooling.

**Nobody agrees on aggregate boundaries, and getting it wrong is expensive to fix.** In CRUD, a wrong table boundary is a migration. In event sourcing, your aggregate boundary determines your consistency boundary, your event stream granularity, and your replay cost — baked into every event you've already written. Splitting or merging an aggregate after the fact means rewriting history or living with a boundary you know is wrong.

**Reads are a second system you now have to build and keep in sync.** Event sourcing answers "what happened," not "what's true right now" — you need projections/read models (CQRS, whether you call it that or not) for anything you'd normally just query. That's a second data model, a sync mechanism, eventual consistency bugs (user does X, refreshes, doesn't see X yet), and projection rebuilds that take real engineering effort when logic changes. Teams that adopt event sourcing "for the audit log" often didn't budget for building a whole read-side.

**Replay performance becomes a production problem, not a tutorial footnote.** Aggregates with long histories get slow to rehydrate. You need snapshotting, and snapshotting introduces its own versioning and invalidation problems layered on top of the event versioning problem above.

**Debugging and tooling are genuinely worse.** "Why is this record wrong" becomes "replay the last 40,000 events and reason about what mutated state at each step," often with no good tooling — you're building your own event store admin, replay debugger, inspection queries. Most shops don't have this and feel the gap the first time there's a production incident.

**The team doesn't actually buy in.** This is the real reason both your companies likely stalled. Event sourcing demands everyone touching that domain understand events-as-source-of-truth, command/event separation, and eventual consistency. One engineer who reaches for a mutable update "just this once" corrupts the model's guarantees, and nobody notices until an audit or a replay disagrees with the read model. Mid-implementation abandonment usually means: the team hit the schema-evolution or read-model-cost wall, realized the payoff (audit trail, temporal queries, replay-based debugging) wasn't something the business actually valued enough to justify the tax, and cut losses before sunk cost got worse.

The pattern under all of this: event sourcing trades a class of problems you understand (migrations, query complexity) for a class you don't have institutional experience with (event versioning, replay-based reasoning, dual read/write models) — and the trade only pays off if you genuinely need the audit/temporal/replay properties, not just because it sounded architecturally clean. Most CRUD-shaped businesses don't need it, and discover that around month six.
