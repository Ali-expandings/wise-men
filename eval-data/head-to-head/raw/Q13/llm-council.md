## Council Verdict: Entering a Market Against a 10x Dominant Incumbent

### Where the Council Agrees
Every advisor who engaged with the niche option treated it as a false safety. A niche only stays undefended while it's small and unproven — the moment it shows real traction, it becomes visible and the incumbent either builds the feature in a quarter or acquires you before you're big enough to negotiate. "Underserved segment, no direct fight" is a pitch-deck phrase, not a moat.

Two advisors independently landed on the same diagnostic test from different angles: sort the incumbent's dissatisfaction into **structural** (a tradeoff baked into their pricing, scale, or brand that they cannot undo without hurting their core business) versus **neglect** (something they'd fix in a quarter if it mattered). One framed it as analysis, the other as an executable task — same filter, stated twice. All five peer reviewers ranked one of these two responses highest, and four of five singled out this exact distinction as the sharpest idea on the table.

There's also agreement that unhappy customers are better used as *intelligence* than as a *target list*. Chasing them as customers inherits their churn habit — they'll leave you for the next shiny thing too. Reading their complaints to find the incumbent's structural blind spot is a stronger, different move.

### Where the Council Clashes
The real split: fight inside the incumbent's category, or leave it entirely? Two advisors argue for staying in-category — use complaint data to find a defensible pocket within the existing market. Two others argue this is fighting with 10% of the chips on the incumbent's home field no matter which pocket you pick, and that the only real move is finding where the underlying job is shifting, or targeting people who were never in the market at all (non-consumers priced out or underserved). Staying in-category gives you evidence and a fast, falsifiable test; leaving it avoids a resource-rigged fight but trades certainty for a longer, costlier discovery process with no guaranteed payoff. A fifth advisor stays outside the fight, contributing a sharp cross-cutting test ("is this fight measured on my terms or theirs") without committing to a side — useful as a skeptic, but peer review flagged that its own conclusion is just as underspecified as the option it criticized.

### Blind Spots the Council Caught
Every reviewer, independently, flagged the same gap: nobody priced the capital and time cost of running the diagnostic itself against the stated resource constraint. "Go read the churn data" sounds cheap in a paragraph but burns real runway — and nobody set a budget, a deadline, or a kill-switch for what happens if the data comes back ambiguous.

Multiple reviewers independently caught the same weakness in the category-expansion pitch: building demand from non-consumers is usually more capital- and time-intensive than niche-carving or defector-poaching — close to disqualifying against a "limited resources" constraint the advisor never reconciled with its own recommendation.

Nobody addressed what happens *after* you win the opening move. Every response optimized for entry; none for defensibility — what stops the incumbent, or the next scrappy entrant, from doing to you exactly what you just did to them. Also absent: real unit economics/CAC comparison across the three paths, and whether you can even reach the target segment through available distribution before running out of money.

### The Recommendation
Run the structural-vs-neglect diagnostic first, and let its output — not a pre-committed identity as "the defector company" or "the niche company" — decide where you land. Chase the specific, recurring complaint that traces back to something the incumbent structurally cannot fix (pricing architecture, tech stack, sales motion, org chart), because that's the one class of weakness that survives contact with 10x resources; a "fixable in a quarter" weakness doesn't. If that structural gap is a pocket of dissatisfied switchers, go after them — as evidence-backed conviction, not loyalty. If it instead reveals a segment the incumbent's model can't reach at all, you've found real upside with evidence behind it instead of a hypothesis. Pure niche-hunting alone is off the table as a first move — open-ended, no natural endpoint, and inherently at risk of being copied the moment it's proven, unless the structural test first confirms the incumbent genuinely can't follow you there.

Time-box the diagnostic hard — it's a real cost against your 10% of the resources — and if it comes back ambiguous, that's itself a signal to walk away from a head-on entry into this market, not a default fallback to chasing unhappy customers anyway.

### The One Thing to Do First
Pull 100 of the incumbent's 1–2 star reviews (G2, Reddit, app store), tag every complaint, isolate whichever one recurs in 30%+ of them, and check it against anything publicly knowable about the incumbent's pricing model, tech architecture, or org structure to determine whether it's structural or fixable-in-a-quarter — one week deadline, with a predefined exit: if nothing structural turns up, head-on entry into this market is off the table.
