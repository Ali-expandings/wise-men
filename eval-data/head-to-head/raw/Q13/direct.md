Neither alone. Both are tactics; the strategy is picking a niche where the incumbent's size actively works against them, then using unhappy customers as your wedge into it — not as the whole play.

**Why "chase the unhappy customers" alone fails.** Every market leader has a churn tail — people annoyed by price, bloat, support, whatever. Going after them directly means competing on the same battlefield the incumbent already dominates. If your wedge is copyable (better UX, lower price, faster support), a 10x-resourced player just matches it. You end up doing free market research for them, and they either fix the gap, cut price to zero-margin you out, or acquire you. Dissatisfaction is a symptom to mine for insight, not a strategy to build on.

**Why "find an ignored niche" is usually right — but only if the neglect is structural.** The question to ask about any niche: *why* does the leader ignore it? Two very different answers:

- *Execution gap* — they just haven't gotten around to it, or do it lazily. Weak moat. Once you prove the niche is worth money, they'll turn a fraction of their resources on it and roll over you.
- *Structural gap* — serving that niche would cannibalize their core revenue, conflict with their existing customer base, break their pricing/sales model, or require an org structure they can't rebuild without blowing up what makes them the leader. This is the innovator's-dilemma case. They can see you coming and still can't follow, because doing so hurts their core business more than losing the niche does.

Only the second kind is a real beachhead. That's the Geoffrey Moore / guerrilla-marketing playbook: pick the smallest niche you can dominate completely where the leader is structurally handcuffed, own it end to end (word of mouth, category-defining, not just "cheaper alternative"), then expand adjacent from a position of actual strength rather than trying to out-resource them head-on from day one.

**Where unhappy customers fit in properly:** use them as intel, not as the target market. Talk to churned/dissatisfied users to find the *job* the incumbent structurally can't or won't serve — that's how you find the real niche, as opposed to guessing. If their complaints trace back to something core to the incumbent's business model, that's your niche. If their complaints trace back to sloppy execution, that's a mirage — fix-able by the incumbent in a quarter.

**Practical filter before committing:** for any candidate niche, ask "if we win this completely, does the incumbent responding cost them more than it costs us to be there?" If yes — commit hard, single-niche focus, ignore the temptation to go broad early. If no — it's not a beachhead, it's a feature request with your name on it.

One thing to watch: don't confuse "smaller/underserved" with "structurally protected." Small-but-copyable niches get steamrolled the moment they show traction; the incumbent's 10x resources make them fast followers, not just brand-strong ones.
