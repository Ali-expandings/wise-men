❓ **Q1** - **Nature of the incumbent's moat**: Is their dominance network-effects-based (product gets more valuable as more people use it, making attack near-impossible), or is it scale/cost, brand/trust, or distribution/regulatory lock-in (attackable via focus)?

➡️ Recommended: Treat it as brand + scale advantage, not a hard network effect. "10x resources and strong brand" describes a well-capitalized incumbent, not a platform where value compounds with users — that's a fundamentally different (much harder) fight.

✅ Assumed: Brand/scale moat, not network effects.

---

❓ **Q2** - **Market growth stage**: Is this market still growing (new customers entering) or mature/saturated (you can only win by taking existing customers away)?

➡️ Recommended: Assume growing/expanding. Framing this as "entering a market" rather than "disrupting a fixed pie" implies room for new demand — which favors niche and greenfield plays over head-on customer theft.

✅ Assumed: Growing market.

---

❓ **Q3** - **Your unfair advantage**: What do you have that the incumbent structurally can't match quickly — speed of iteration, founder/domain expertise, a cost structure edge, or willingness to serve a segment that's uneconomical for them?

➡️ Recommended: Assume speed and focus — the standard small-team-vs-incumbent edge: faster iteration, willingness to go deep on a segment too small or too specific for a company optimizing for its mainstream base.

✅ Assumed: Speed/focus advantage, not capital or brand.

---

❓ **Q4** - **Dissatisfaction signal**: Is there real evidence of unhappy incumbent customers (reviews, churn, support complaints), or is the incumbent broadly well-loved, with only a thin, low-value sliver of detractors?

➡️ Recommended: Assume moderate, minority dissatisfaction — enough to be useful as a signal of where they're weak, not enough to be a standalone strategy. A truly hated incumbent wouldn't be described as having "a strong brand."

✅ Assumed: Real but minority dissatisfaction — useful as a diagnostic, not a customer-acquisition engine on its own.

---

❓ **Q5** - **Beachhead definition**: Should the niche be picked by vertical/use-case, by customer size (e.g. SMB the incumbent under-serves while chasing enterprise, or vice versa), or by a specific values/feature gap (price, privacy, service, speed)?

➡️ Recommended: Don't treat "niche" and "unhappy customers" as competing options — merge them. Define the niche at the intersection of an underserved segment AND the specific pain point driving Q4's dissatisfied minority. That way you're not fighting for random defectors; you're owning a slice the incumbent structurally can't or won't serve well.

✅ Assumed: Niche = underserved segment defined by the incumbent's specific weak point.

---

❓ **Q6** - **Confrontation posture**: Market directly against the incumbent (comparison ads, "switch from X") or grow quietly under their radar until you have real share?

➡️ Recommended: Stay under the radar early. Direct comparison marketing is the fastest way to get a 10x-resourced competitor to notice and respond — price cuts, feature-matching, marketing spend you can't outlast. Win the niche quietly first.

✅ Assumed: No direct confrontation until a defensible base exists.

---

❓ **Q7** - **Expansion path**: After winning the beachhead, expand horizontally into many niches/the broad market at once, or go deep — dominate adjacent needs within the same niche before moving outward?

➡️ Recommended: Classic beachhead-then-adjacent expansion. Fully own niche #1 (high share, high loyalty) before spreading resources into contesting the core market — premature broadening is how under-resourced challengers get squeezed.

✅ Assumed: Sequential expansion from a fully-owned beachhead, not simultaneous multi-front growth.

---

❓ **Q8** - **Pricing posture**: Undercut the incumbent to accelerate switching, match their price but bundle superior fit/service, or price as a premium specialist?

➡️ Recommended: Price at parity or a premium, not below. A price war against 10x resources is a war of attrition you lose; a focused player earns premium pricing through superior fit for the niche, not through being cheaper.

✅ Assumed: Parity/premium pricing, no undercutting.

---

❓ **Q9** - **Exit criteria for the beachhead phase**: What signals "niche won, ready to expand" — market share % within the niche, retention/NPS relative to the incumbent, or a revenue threshold?

➡️ Recommended: Dominant share and retention within the defined niche (e.g., clear plurality/majority share plus retention or NPS visibly better than the incumbent's in that niche) — not a revenue number. Share and loyalty prove the position is defensible before you spend resources expanding.

✅ Assumed: Share + retention dominance within the niche is the trigger to expand.

---

**Final recommendation**

Don't chase unhappy customers as a standalone strategy, and don't pick an arbitrary "ignored" niche either — merge the two. Define a beachhead segment at the intersection of (a) a customer group the incumbent structurally under-serves given its brand/scale-optimized model, and (b) the specific pain points already driving a minority of its customers to complain. That's a niche worth owning, not a niche for its own sake.

Execute it quietly: no head-on comparison marketing, no price war — both invite a 10x-resourced player to respond with force it can sustain and you can't. Price at parity or a premium, justified by superior fit, not by being cheaper. Win that niche outright — dominant share and better retention/NPS than the incumbent achieves there — before expanding into adjacent segments. Only engage the incumbent directly, if ever, once that base is defensible.
