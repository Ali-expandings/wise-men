## Recommendation

Neither "go after their unhappy customers" nor "find an ignored niche" is the strategy — both are where you look, not what protects you. Enter a niche through a business-model or delivery asymmetry the incumbent can't cheaply copy without damaging its own revenue, channel, or brand, and use their unhappy customers only as the signal for where that niche is. All four council members converged on this independently, from four different frameworks.

## Why

- **Both options as stated fail the "can they just copy you" test.** A gap that's merely under-attended, not structurally costly for the incumbent to close, disappears the moment you prove it's worth money. All four members cited the same cautionary pattern: Instagram cloning Stories, Microsoft bundling Teams for free — an edge with no structural cost to copy is a feature, not a strategy.
- **"Unhappy customers" is a diagnostic, not a target list.** Most vocal complainers don't actually switch — they voice frustration and stay, absent a trigger like a renewal, price hike, or outage. The ones who would switch are visible in the incumbent's own churn data, so a broad conquest push gets neutralized by targeted retention discounts before you build share. Use complaint patterns to find where the incumbent is structurally weak; don't treat them as a customer-acquisition channel on their own.
- **A niche only protects you if the incumbent is structurally disincentivized from following, not just currently inattentive.** It has to be a rounding error for them and a real business for you — unprofitable, channel-conflicting, or brand-contradicting at their scale. If it's simple oversight, your traction is what ends the oversight.
- **The real lever, named four separate ways independently, is the same mechanism**: counter-positioning (a model they'd cannibalize their core to copy), low-end/new-market disruption (a job they're not structurally built to serve profitably), a go-to-market wedge (a channel or pricing motion their cost structure can't cheaply mirror), and a "cannibalization trap" (undercutting a practice that funds their margin, so matching you costs them across their *entire* base). Practical filter one member proposed: *if they matched this across their whole customer base tomorrow, would it cost them more than it threatens us?* If no, keep looking.
- **It's the only path that fits a 1/10th-resource position.** An outspend fight for their unhappy customers (migration support, comparison-term bidding, retention counter-offers) favors whoever has more cash — not you. A structurally protected niche wins on organic/community/self-serve motion, stays under their attention threshold while you build it, and compounds into switching costs and references instead of a one-time account swap.

## Tradeoffs and risks

- **Speed vs. durability.** Pure unhappy-customer conquest is the fastest way to early revenue, but it's revenue the incumbent can buy back. The council's sequencing suggestion: use narrow, named unhappy-customer targeting *after* the structural niche has proof points to sell with — not as the opening move.
- **The asymmetry has to be real, not assumed.** The single biggest risk flagged across all four reports: mistaking a temporarily-ignored segment or an easily-cloned feature for a structural moat. If their engineers could replicate your edge in a sprint at no cost to their own business, you don't have a strategy yet.
- **The answer is conditional on facts this question didn't include, and the council was unanimous on which ones matter most**: what kind of moat the incumbent has (network effects push you toward a *dense* sub-network beachhead, not just a small one); whether the unhappiness is chiefly about price (points toward low-end disruption specifically); your sales-cycle/switching-cost environment; and whether the business is software-like (self-serve/PLG works) or physical, regulated, or capital-intensive (re-express the same asymmetry as geography, a specialist channel, or a distribution partnership instead).
- **Time horizon matters.** If the goal is a near-term sale rather than a durable business, a visible niche irritant can be "enough" even without a fully structural moat, since acquirers pay for the threat you represent. Treat that as the exception, not the base case.
- **Confidence: moderate, and consistent across all four members (50–75%).** The structural framing itself is held with reasonable conviction; what's genuinely uncertain is whether a defensible asymmetry exists in your specific market — that can only be resolved by testing it there, not in the abstract.

## Final call

Don't launch a broad "steal their unhappy customers" campaign, and don't just quietly serve whatever segment currently looks unattended. Run the diagnostic first: map what it would cost the incumbent — in cannibalized revenue, channel conflict, or brand contradiction — to match you. Where their customers' complaints trace back to something fixable, that's not your opening. Where complaints trace back to something structural to how the incumbent makes money, that's your beachhead. Take the smallest such segment that's real money for you and a rounding error for them, enter it through a channel, pricing model, or delivery motion they can't cheaply mirror, and hold off on broader unhappy-customer conquest until that beachhead has given you reference customers and switching costs to sell with.
