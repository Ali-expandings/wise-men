## Your obligations

Almost certainly none, formally. A few caveats:

- If you're in a management, HR, or compliance role with a duty to report discrimination you become aware of, that changes things — check your employee handbook / employment contract for a reporting duty clause.
- If you're a manager of any of the affected women, you may have a duty of care even without a formal policy — turning a blind eye as their manager is a different posture than as a peer.
- Otherwise: you have no legal duty to act. This is a moral/values call, not a compliance one.

What you *do* have, in most US jurisdictions, is protection if you choose to act. Title VII's opposition clause protects employees from retaliation for opposing a practice they reasonably believe is discriminatory — this protection isn't limited to the people directly harmed. So raising it, in good faith, through proper channels, is protected activity even though it's not your pay at stake.

## What you can actually do

**1. Put it in writing, again, and go over HR's head.** Six months of silence means the first report died somewhere. Re-raise it in writing (email, not verbal) to HR leadership and, if there's one, your company's ethics/compliance function or the audit committee if it's public. Writing creates a timestamped record and removes "we never heard about this" as a defense later.

**2. Be careful about the data itself.** How you learned this matters. If you saw it through your normal job duties (e.g., you're in finance/ops and legitimately have payroll visibility), you're on solid ground. If you accessed something you weren't supposed to, raising the *issue* is still fine, but redistributing specific numbers could create a separate problem for you (confidentiality/policy violation) — stick to describing the pattern, not leaking individual salaries.

**3. External options if internal channels stall again:**
- **EEOC / state fair employment agency** — a charge can be filed by someone other than the victim in some cases, or you can encourage affected colleagues to file (charges are free, and retaliation against them for filing is separately illegal).
- **State labor department** — many states (NY, CA, CO, WA, IL, MA, others) have pay transparency or pay data reporting laws with their own complaint mechanisms, sometimes faster than EEOC.
- **Employment attorney** — a 30-minute consult (many offer free initial calls) can tell you whether what you've seen rises to an actionable pattern, and whether *you* have any exposure for how you obtained the data.

**4. Support the people actually affected, don't act unilaterally on their behalf.** The most useful thing you can do as an ally is often not "I go fix this for them" but "I make sure they know this exists and back them if they choose to act" — pay discrimination claims are personal, and some colleagues may not want it pursued the way you'd pursue it. Loop them in factually, without pressure, and let them decide whether to escalate personally or via counsel.

**5. If you're senior enough to have influence, use it directly.** A pattern like this, sat on for six months, is a real legal and reputational liability for the company. Framing it that way to leadership — "this is a liability, not just an HR ticket" — often moves faster than a values appeal.

**Bottom line:** you're not obligated to do anything, but you're protected if you do. The highest-leverage move is a written escalation past HR to whoever owns risk (legal/compliance/board), paired with quietly making sure affected colleagues know their options exist — without deciding for them how hard to push.
