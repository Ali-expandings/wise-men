Not a legal opinion — talk to an employment lawyer for specifics in your jurisdiction. Here's the general shape.

**Your legal obligations: minimal, usually none.** As an individual employee who isn't a manager, HR, or otherwise responsible for compliance, you generally have no personal legal duty to report this. You're not liable for pay discrimination you didn't create or approve. The exception: if you're a manager/supervisor with authority over pay or personnel decisions, some employers' policies (and in some cases, your role itself) create a duty to escalate known violations — worth checking your own job description and any manager handbook.

**What's actually illegal here (US frame, adjust for your country):** Federal Equal Pay Act and Title VII (and most state equal-pay laws, many stricter) prohibit paying women less than men for substantially equal work. "HR knows and did nothing for 6 months" doesn't create new liability by itself, but it undercuts any later claim the company acted "in good faith" once affected employees file.

**Protections that matter for you specifically:**
- Retaliation protections under Title VII, the EPA, and most state laws cover you even though you're not personally harmed — they protect anyone who reports, opposes, or participates in an investigation of discriminatory pay practices. Retaliation against a bystander who reports is still illegal.
- Discussing pay/wages with coworkers is protected concerted activity under the NLRA (private sector) — the company can't lawfully punish you for talking about this.

**What you can actually do, roughly in order of escalation:**
1. Raise it again with HR in writing (email, not verbal) — creates a paper trail and a clear "on notice" date. Ask what's being done and by when.
2. Escalate to your manager or their manager, or to whoever HR reports to (if you have skip-level access), framing it as a compliance/legal-risk issue.
3. If there's a formal ethics hotline / compliance reporting channel, use it — many companies treat that channel differently than a casual HR complaint.
4. External: file a charge with the EEOC (or your state's fair employment agency) — you don't have to be the victim to report discriminatory practices you've witnessed, though EEOC charges are normally filed by affected individuals or via a class/pattern-or-practice referral. A stronger move is often encouraging affected coworkers to file, since they have standing and stronger claims.
5. Document everything now — dates, what you found, who you told, HR's response. If this ever becomes a legal matter, contemporaneous notes from a non-affected witness are valuable.

**Practical note:** the people with the most legal leverage are the affected women themselves. If you have a good relationship with any of them, letting them know what you found (carefully, without violating any confidentiality you're bound by) lets them decide whether to pursue it — they have standing you don't, and pay transparency about your own or others' wages is generally protected activity for them too.
