## Decision / Answer
You have essentially no legal duty to act further as a non-affected bystander (absent a management/compliance role, which you haven't indicated). Re-filing the identical complaint through the same HR channel that has sat on this for six months is very likely a wasted move. Six of seven council members — reasoning independently from duty ethics, stakeholder rights, decision strategy, relational impact, employment-law incentives, and contrarian institutional-pattern analysis — converged on the same first move without being told to: before you escalate on anyone's behalf, privately find out whether the affected women already know, what they want done, and whether they want your help at all. Then follow their lead, including if that lead is "do nothing for now."

The council's highest-rated answer goes a step further and should shape your actual plan. Escalating further through formal channels (HR again, general counsel, an EEOC-type charge) risks converting a live pay problem into a contained legal matter — once counsel is involved, the compensation data the women would eventually need for their own claim tends to get wrapped in privilege, and a complaint from a non-comparator is procedurally easy for HR to close without remediating anything. Six months of silence is consistent with either stonewalling or a slow privileged pay-equity audit, and from outside you can't tell which — escalating blind risks hardening a defensive posture that was about to resolve, or accomplishing nothing against one that wasn't.

Concretely:
1. Privately tell at least one affected colleague what you found and how, and ask what she wants — including "nothing, for now." Do this before drafting any complaint, email, or charge.
2. If invited, act as a dated, factual, corroborating witness for their process rather than filing your own parallel complaint — once you're "the guy who filed," your later corroboration reads as motivated rather than neutral.
3. Meanwhile, use the leverage you already legitimately have inside compensation decisions: advocate for specific raises on the merits, refuse to lowball counteroffers, disclose your own pay when asked so a real comparator exists.
4. Only escalate formally (above HR, external agency, legal) if and when the affected women want that pursued — and go in support of their process, not as an independent complainant. (Procedurally, a third party *can* file an EEOC-type charge on someone's behalf under 29 CFR 1601.7 — I verified this — but the agency still confirms the affected person's authorization directly, so their buy-in is required either way, not just advisable.)

**Cost of being wrong**: if this caution is wrong and it's really pure stonewalling, waiting to consult the women first costs some time — but that's recoverable; you can still escalate later, with more evidence and their backing. If you instead escalate now without asking and the council is right, the cost is less recoverable: you may burn your value as a neutral witness, trigger a defensive/privileged posture, and expose the women to scrutiny on a timeline they didn't choose, on a matter that is rightly theirs to time.
