## Recommendation

Don't pick from the obvious menu — stay silent, complain to HR again, go to a regulator, or quietly cheer from the sidelines — until you've done the one thing that changes which of those is even right: talk privately with the affected women and find out what they already know and want. Then document what you personally know, and offer yourself as evidence and a witness rather than as their unrequested spokesperson. You almost certainly have no general legal duty to do more than that; you do have a real, if informal, obligation not to sit on information that would help, and not to act on their behalf without asking first.

## Why

Unless you manage people, work in HR or payroll, or hold a compliance or officer-level role — none of which the question states — employment-discrimination law generally puts the duty to fix systematic pay inequity on the employer, not on a coworker who happens to find out about it. What the law generally does instead is protect you if you act: broadly, opposing or supporting a complaint about sex-based pay discrimination is protected activity, and that protection isn't contingent on being personally affected (general shape of US Title VII/Equal Pay Act-type frameworks and their rough equivalents elsewhere — unverified against your specific jurisdiction; confirm with your own employment lawyer, not company counsel). That protection is real but reactive: it gives you a claim after retaliation happens, not a shield that stops it, and quiet reprisals are hard to prove. If your role turns out to include any people-management, HR, payroll, or compliance responsibility, this changes — company policy and, in some places, the law itself can convert "knew and said nothing" into an actual duty, so confirm that fact about yourself before anything else.

Six months of visible inaction feels like proof the company has decided to do nothing. It isn't reliable proof of that. Compensation fixes are frequently run quietly through legal counsel and applied at a scheduled pay review, which looks identical to inaction from where a bystander sits — and you don't know whether "HR knows" means a tracked, substantiated case or an informal, unconfirmed impression. That cuts both ways: it's not a reason to do nothing, but it is a reason not to treat the six months as settled evidence of bad faith when you decide what to do next.

The point most worth acting on is one the question's own framing skips entirely: the choice isn't really between four options, it's whether you act on the women's behalf before or after finding out what they want. Speaking for people without asking first is a real failure mode even when the intent is good — it can expose specific women in a small department to scrutiny they didn't choose, duplicate or undercut a quieter approach they may already be running, and shift the story onto you instead of the actual problem. It also has a practical cost: if you're pushing the same channel that already sat on this for six months, you may be pushing on a part of the org that was never the blocker — the budget to close a pay gap often sits with whoever controls compensation spend, not with HR itself, which is one reason a well-meaning nudge to the same people can go nowhere twice.

## What to do

1. **Today (about 30 minutes, no cost).** Write a dated, personal record — on your own device, off company systems — of what you actually know: how you learned it, what HR was told and when, and anything HR said back. This costs you nothing and preserves something only you currently hold.
2. **This week (about an hour).** Have one or two private, low-pressure conversations with affected colleagues you trust. Ask what they already know, whether they've raised it themselves, gotten advice, or have something in motion you can't see, and — the key question — what they want from you, if anything. If your role is comparable to theirs, telling them your own pay may hand them something they don't have (many workplaces make pay comparison hard to get to on your own).
3. **In parallel, confirm your own footing.** Do you manage anyone, or touch HR, payroll, or compliance? That changes whether you owe more than a moral obligation and how protected you are if you speak up. A confidential first consult with your own employment lawyer — not company counsel — is the fastest way to find out where you actually stand before you're exposed at all; many offer a free or low-cost initial call.
4. **Let their answer, not a fixed script, set your next move.** If they're already handling it and don't want more involvement, document and stand by — don't push. If they want a witness or someone to corroborate what happened, offer that explicitly, in writing, without disclosing anyone's individual pay without consent. If they want it pushed harder internally, use a channel that hasn't already failed for six months — legal or compliance, or a level above whoever has been sitting on it, not HR again.
5. **Treat anything bigger — a formal written complaint, or supporting a claim with a regulator — as theirs to choose, not yours to start.** Offer it; don't lead it, unless they've told you they want that and are choosing not to go first themselves out of the same fear that's kept this quiet for six months. If, after you've asked and a further pay review cycle passes with genuinely no movement, they still want it pushed and are unable or afraid to be the one to do it, a written note on your own account to Legal or Compliance (not HR again) — stating plainly what you know and that you're raising a good-faith concern — is a defensible next step precisely because it comes after you checked, not before.

Stop adding pressure the moment the women tell you they don't want it. Escalate your own documented concern in writing if a further pay review cycle passes with no visible movement and the women are either asking for that or staying silent out of fear rather than choice — that's the point where staying quiet stops being deference and starts being avoidance.

## Risks of this plan

- Talking to colleagues or writing anything down can still reach the wrong ears in a small department; keep records off company systems and don't name anyone without their consent.
- You may be reading "no visible change" as proof of stalling when a quiet fix is already in motion — check before you escalate on that assumption.
- If it turns out you have any management, HR, payroll, or compliance role, the "no legal duty" half of this changes, and some of the personal protection may not apply the same way — confirm this first.
- Retaliation protection only helps after the fact and is hard to prove when it's subtle (a stalled promotion, being quietly left out); it is not a reason to treat speaking up as risk-free.
- Waiting has its own cost: pay-discrimination claims are typically subject to filing deadlines and limits on how far back lost pay can be recovered (unverified specifics — this varies by jurisdiction and is worth confirming with a lawyer, but the direction is consistent: later is worse for their recovery, not yours) — the cost of doing nothing is not zero just because it's not visible to you.

The plan above is reversible at every step through the conversation and the lawyer consult — nothing there commits you to anything public or irreversible. The one step that's harder to walk back is putting a written concern in front of Legal or Compliance on your own account; if that turns out to be premature (the women had a plan, or the issue was already resolving), the recovery path is to say so plainly and let it stand as a documented concern rather than a complaint — awkward, not catastrophic.

## Strongest counter-position

The case for doing distinctly less than a staged escalation plan implies: an employee with no management role has no obligation to do more than make himself available as a comparison point, and the honest, safest version of "getting involved" stops there. Six months of no visible change is weak evidence of anything — companies routinely run pay fixes quietly through counsel and apply them at a scheduled review, so "nothing changed" may just mean "you can't see it." A raw department-level pay gap, looked at without controlling for role, level, and tenure, frequently shrinks or disappears — escalating on an uncontrolled read of it risks burning credibility and exposing colleagues over a gap that turns out to be partly explainable. On this view, the right move is: share your own pay privately, state plainly that you'll corroborate if ever asked, and then genuinely step back — not "step back until the next trigger," but stop, on the view that a bystander deciding when the next escalation trigger fires is still the bystander running the timeline, just on a longer fuse. This position wins if the affected women, once they have the comparison, choose not to act — in which case further pushing by the bystander substitutes his judgment for theirs about a risk that falls mainly on them. What would show it's right: the women, informed, decline further involvement, or a closer look at the pay data shows the gap is substantially explained by legitimate factors once controlled. What would show the staged plan is right instead: the women want support they can't safely provide themselves, or the gap holds up under a fair comparison and simply hasn't been fixed.
