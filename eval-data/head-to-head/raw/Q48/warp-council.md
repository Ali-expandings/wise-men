## Recommendation

He has no general legal duty to fix or report this — unless he's a manager, has any influence over the affected women's pay, or sits in HR/comp/legal, in which case the duty is real and his own exposure is higher. But he has a genuine ethical case for action, and his comparatively low personal risk is itself a reason to use it — in *support* of the affected women, not in front of them. Concretely: verify what he actually knows this week, send one precise written status-and-timeline request to HR, give it a real but bounded window, and escalate above HR in writing only if that produces silence or deflection rather than a credible plan — running all of this in parallel with, and led by, what the affected women themselves want.

## Why

- All three members converged independently on the same shape despite non-overlapping angles: no blanket legal duty for an individual contributor, but a real policy duty and sharper legal stakes if he's a manager or has comp influence; retaliation protection attaches to what he *does* (a specific, good-faith, reasonably-conveyed complaint) not to merely knowing; and "HR knows, nothing's changed in 6 months" is genuinely ambiguous — real pay-equity remediation (privileged legal review, budget/comp-cycle timing) often takes many months in good faith, so the diagnostic is the *quality* of HR's answer to a direct ask, not the elapsed time alone.
- A single precise written ask converts an unfalsifiable grievance into a timestamped, answerable record: either a credible process with an owner and timeline (stand down, recheck later) or documented stonewalling (now he has grounds and a paper trail to escalate).
- It keeps the legal claim — which only the affected women can pursue — in their hands, while using his lower-risk position for what it's actually good for: written, factual pressure and an offer to corroborate, not speaking for or over them.
- The contrarian report's sharpest point reshapes the "why" rather than just softening it: "systematically underpaying" needs to survive an actual like-for-like comparison (role, level, tenure, performance) before he repeats it as fact. A department-wide average can conflate an unlawful pay gap with a separate leveling/promotion problem, and overstating it both weakens his own retaliation protection — which depends on a reasonable, good-faith belief — and gives a skeptical company an easy way to dismiss him.

## Tradeoffs and risks

- **Overreach risk:** naming affected colleagues or their figures without consent, escalating externally or publicly before internal channels are actually exhausted, or becoming the self-appointed face of a claim that isn't his — any of these can identify the women against their wishes, push the company into a defensive legal posture that slows the real fix, and shift credit and risk onto him instead of them.
- **Inaction risk:** the gap compounds every cycle through raises, bonuses, and retirement contributions, and the oldest recoverable back pay ages out month by month (each new paycheck restarts the filing clock, so delay isn't fatal to their claim, but it isn't free). If he has any influence over their pay or sits in HR/comp/legal, continuing to administer the status quo after he "knows" is where his own exposure turns real.
- **Evidence risk:** if what he "discovered" is closer to rumor or an unadjusted average than a verified, controlled comparison, tighten that before putting anything in writing — both because it's fairer to the women and the company, and because weak evidence undercuts the legal protection he'd otherwise have.
- **Process risk:** HR's silence may be privileged legal review and budget-cycle mechanics rather than stonewalling. Escalating on impatience alone burns capital he may need later and can misread a slow-but-real process as a dead one.
- **Jurisdiction risk:** all of the above defaults to general US private-sector law because no country or state was given; exact deadlines, whistleblower statutes, and pay-transparency rules vary significantly by state and are materially different outside the US (the council flagged the UK Equality Act 2010 and the EU Pay Transparency Directive as the non-US reference points). None of this substitutes for one paid consult with an employment attorney in his actual jurisdiction — every member independently recommended this as cheap and close to essential if he's a manager or touched the data through privileged access.

## Final call

**Do now (this week to the next few weeks):**

1. Write a private, dated log of exactly what he knows, how he learned it, and what "HR knows" actually consisted of — kept off company systems. Stop calling it "systematic" until it survives an actual like-for-like comparison.
2. Check the employee handbook / code of conduct for any reporting duty, and get one attorney consult — near-mandatory if he's a manager, influences pay, or works in HR/comp/legal; cheap insurance otherwise.
3. Talk to the affected women before escalating further: ask what they know and want, don't disclose anyone else's figures without their consent, and offer concrete support (witness, corroboration, joint complaint) rather than deciding for them.
4. Send one precise written follow-up to HR asking directly whether it's under active review, what the process is, and for a rough timeline. This is the single highest-leverage move — the answer, or its absence, is the actual diagnostic for everything after it.
5. Give that a real but bounded window (roughly 30 days, or the next relevant pay cycle). A credible owner/process/timeline → stand down and calendar a recheck. Silence, deflection, or defensiveness → escalate in writing above HR (Legal/Compliance, ethics hotline, or skip-level), explicitly referencing the unanswered ask.

**Optional / later, and mostly not his to spend:** helping affected colleagues toward their own attorney consult or an EEOC/state-agency filing if internal channels are genuinely exhausted. His role there is corroborating witness, not filer or spokesperson — the claim and the choice of when to go external belong to the women, not to him.
