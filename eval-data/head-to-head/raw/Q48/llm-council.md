## Council Verdict: HR Knows, Nothing's Changed — What Now

### Where the Council Agrees
Every advisor converges on the legal baseline: knowing about the pay gap creates no personal legal liability for him. That's settled, not a live worry. Four of five advisors also independently land on the same reframe — HR's six months of silence is diagnostic data, not a closed door. Whether they call it "the system failing" or "HR doing its actual job (protecting the company, not fixing pay)," they agree the next move isn't re-poking HR, it's going over or around it: legal/compliance, whoever HR answers to, or an external agency. And there's real convergence on the leverage point: his lack of personal stake isn't a diminished claim to speak, it's what makes him credible — he can't be waved off as a self-interested complainant the way the women could be.

### Where the Council Clashes
The real fight is about the affected women's agency, and it splits the panel down the middle. The Executor treats them as a resource to loop in once other levers are exhausted — a later-stage tactic. The Contrarian, Outsider, and all five peer reviews treat that as close to a first-order ethical failure: he doesn't know if they know, what they want, or whether quiet job-hunting is their actual strategy, and unilateral escalation (especially external/EEOC) risks outing them or torching a plan they didn't ask him to run. This isn't a minor quibble — it's a disagreement about whether his good intentions entitle him to act on their behalf without asking first. The council's peer review round makes clear: asking them belongs near the front, not buried in a step-4 fallback.

There's a smaller clash on posture. The First Principles Thinker and Expansionist frame this as high-agency, almost duty-bound — he's not a bystander, act now, document now. The Outsider pushes back hard: you don't actually know why HR stalled (disbelief, cost, or invisible remediation in progress), and treating silence as proof of bad faith before checking is itself sloppy. Reasonable people disagree here because the cost of guessing wrong runs in opposite directions: escalate on a false read of stonewalling and you've burned trust and maybe outed people over nothing; wait for clarity that never comes and you've spent another quarter while the gap compounds.

### Blind Spots the Council Caught
Peer review surfaced things no single advisor said outright. Nobody named that state law is the actual hinge variable — pay transparency and anti-retaliation protections vary enormously by jurisdiction, so the honest "first move" may just be a free consult with an employment lawyer before anything else. Nobody flagged the confidentiality problem in the Executor's own plan: pulling comp data he wasn't authorized to see and using it to go to legal or the women themselves could itself violate policy. Nobody suggested the lowest-risk middle rung: an anonymous ethics hotline or audit-committee tip sits between "wait on HR" and "call a lawyer / go public," and none of the five raised it. And multiple reviews caught that HR's inaction could have a mundane, defensible explanation — a remediation plan without back pay creates its own disparate-impact exposure, or the fix is sitting in a comp-cycle budget nobody communicated — which nobody probed before recommending escalation.

### The Recommendation
Escalate past HR, but sequence it correctly: verify before you amplify, and consult before you act for others.

The council's majority view is right on the macro shape — six months of silence is enough to conclude HR is not going to be the fix, and "not personally affected" is an asset, not a disqualifier. He should escalate beyond HR. But two things must happen before "escalate" turns into "go to legal/board/agency": first, get one clarifying answer out of HR or his manager — specifically what is blocking action and what the timeline is — since that single question resolves the ambiguity (bad faith vs. slow remediation vs. miscommunication) cheaply. Second, and non-negotiable: before any move that could reveal who's affected or trigger a formal process (legal, EEOC, board), he needs to find a way to check with the women themselves — even just one trusted colleague among them — about whether they want this pushed, and on what timeline. Skipping that step converts an ally into someone making decisions for people who didn't ask him to.

Do not quit, and do not go external (EEOC, press, board) as a first strike — those are last-resort levers after internal escalation with documentation has also stalled.

### The One Thing to Do First
Today, in writing: send HR (from his work email, dated) a short follow-up citing the original report date and six months of no visible action, asking directly what specifically is blocking resolution and what the timeline is — this creates the paper trail everyone agrees he needs, and it's also the fastest way to find out whether HR's silence is bad faith, budget-cycle timing, or legal review, which determines everything he does next.
