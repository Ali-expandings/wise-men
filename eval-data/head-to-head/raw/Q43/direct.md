There's no clean answer, but the dilemma gets easier if you split it into two separate questions you're currently merging: what do you *do*, and what do you *say*.

**What you do:** Quietly shoring up continuity — documenting their knowledge, cross-training a backup, not building next year's plan entirely on their shoulders — is just good management. Do it. But do it the way you'd do it for any key person on a small team, not as a reaction targeted at this one individual. The trap here is letting suspicion curdle into treatment: quietly cutting them out of the interesting project, holding back the stretch assignment "just in case," going a little cold. That's where it becomes self-fulfilling and unfair — if your read is wrong, you've demoted someone in practice for no reason; if your read is right, you've just given them a cleaner reason to leave and a story about why. Keep giving them real work and honest feedback until they actually tell you otherwise.

**What you say:** Don't confront it directly ("I know you're job hunting"). Best case it goes nowhere good — they deny it and now feel watched, or they confirm it and you've forced a conversation before they were ready to have it, on your timeline not theirs. However you found out, showing your hand rarely buys you anything. What's worth doing instead is the conversation you should be having anyway: in your normal 1:1 cadence, ask how they're feeling about the role, what they want more or less of, what's not working. Not as a trap, genuinely. That gives them an opening to bring it up themselves if they want to, without you making them account for their job search like it's something owed to you.

Because it isn't. Worth naming directly since it sounds like part of what's bothering you: an employee quietly looking around while still doing their job isn't a betrayal. It's the same asymmetric-risk management you'd expect them to do on their end that you're doing on yours with the succession planning. Companies don't pre-announce layoffs as a courtesy either. Reframing it from "they're hiding something from me" to "this is just normal market behavior" will probably do more for how it's bothering you than any conversation with them will.

One more use for the suspicion, separate from whether to act on it interpersonally: treat it as a diagnostic. Is there a real, fixable reason they might be looking — comp behind market, stalled growth, a bad dynamic with you or the team? Worth honestly checking, and fixing if so, regardless of whether this particular read is even correct. That's the one place where "acting on it" is unambiguously right.
