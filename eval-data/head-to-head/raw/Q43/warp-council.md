## Recommendation

Don't confront them about the job search, and don't freeze them out either — fold them into whatever standing career check-ins and continuity practices (documentation, cross-training) you'd run for any key person, without naming what you know, and don't let this tip change what projects or opportunities they get. Go more direct only if they raise it first, it becomes independently obvious, or you have a real, nameable business reason to act — not just your own discomfort.

## Why

- **All three members converged on this**: naming the specific evidence ("I heard you're job searching") is the one move everyone flagged as high-risk in the default case — it usually forces a denial, burns the channel, and teaches your wider team that private signals reach the boss and get acted on.
- **All three also converged**: the real danger isn't which conversation you have, it's letting the knowledge quietly change how you *treat* them — fewer stretch projects, more scrutiny, quiet exclusion. That's the path most likely to look retaliatory later, most likely to become a self-fulfilling prophecy, and, per the methodology angle, the one with real legal exposure if it ever surfaces.
- **Where they split, worth knowing**: methodology and practitioner both land on "have the check-in, just make it a standing/general practice rather than a one-off triggered by the tip." The contrarian pushed back hard that a check-in secretly *caused* by covert knowledge is a "probe disguised as support," and argued it's only more honest than that to the degree it's genuinely part of a recurring practice you'd run anyway — otherwise, do nothing at all unless you have an actual business reason to act. That's less a contradiction than a sharpening: the check-in earns its legitimacy exactly to the degree it's real and routine, not invented this month because of what you heard.
- **On your stated discomfort**: two of three members independently made the same point from different angles — check whether the pull to act is about protecting the business or about relieving your own anxiety at being "out of the loop." Those call for different responses, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which one you're actually solving for before you do anything.

## Tradeoffs and risks

- Confronting directly risks a denial-and-standoff dynamic, especially if your evidence is secondhand (grapevine, a guess from behavior) rather than something they've shown you directly.
- Quiet planning that's specific to this one person — pulling them off the big project, suddenly documenting only their work — is the version that backfires: it's legible, it can read as retaliation, and it can push someone toward the exit who might otherwise have stayed.
- Doing nothing at all forfeits two things: a real chance to fix whatever's driving the search if it's fixable, and any continuity runway if they resign on short notice. It also doesn't actually resolve the thing that's "bothering" you — that discomfort tends to leak into your behavior anyway even when you're trying to act normal.
- Genuine unknowns that would shift this: how you found out (something closer to a direct or semi-direct signal, e.g. a reference-check call, argues for more openness than pure grapevine); how deep they are in the process (early browsing vs. late-stage interviews); how replaceable the role is; and the existing trust level between you two.

## Final call

Do now: keep their workload, projects, and opportunities exactly as they'd otherwise be, and start — or continue — real career check-ins and continuity documentation across your key people generally, not invented for them and not referencing what you heard. Hold off on everything else: don't name the search, don't quietly reroute their work, don't loop in HR pre-emptively. Revisit only if they tell you directly, it becomes obviously undeniable, or an actual business decision (budget, succession, a role transition) forces your hand — at that point, be straightforwardly honest rather than run another disguised check-in.
