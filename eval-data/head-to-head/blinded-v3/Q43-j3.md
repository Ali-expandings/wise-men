## Recommendation

Don't confront your report about the search directly, and don't quietly maneuver around them either. Say nothing about the search itself, but don't simply carry on as if nothing has changed — run the same forward-looking conversation about role, growth, and workload that you should be having with every report on a normal cadence, and keep any continuity or documentation work genuinely team-wide rather than aimed at this one person. Leave disclosure obviously costless if they ever choose to raise it themselves.

## Why

Raising the search directly forces a choice onto them between an unwanted disclosure and a denial — and once you've either heard a lie or made them feel watched, that cost doesn't go away, and it tends to accelerate the exit you were hoping to prevent rather than prevent it. You'd effectively be asking a question where the honest answer is punishable and the dishonest one is free, so silence or denial is the rational response, not evidence you were wrong to suspect it.

Doing absolutely nothing differently forfeits the one real advantage this knowledge gives you — lead time — and risks a scramble if they do leave. But targeted quiet planning (pulling them off a big multi-year bet, quietly excluding them from next year's roadmap, a sudden documentation request aimed only at their work) usually isn't as secret as it feels. In a team small enough that a job search would reach you, it's a team small enough to notice a sudden change in how one person is treated — and discovery of a hidden response reads as retaliation for something they haven't even done, which is a worse rupture than a direct question would have been, precisely because it was hidden.

The version that holds up regardless of whether they're actually leaving is the one where nothing you do depends on the secret staying a secret: a genuine check-in framed the same way you'd frame it for anyone, and continuity practices that are standing team hygiene rather than a reaction to this one person. It's also worth being honest that a real share of what's driving the urge to act here is your own discomfort — being kept out of the loop, not wanting to be blindsided — and that feeling, while legitimate, isn't itself a claim on them. Nothing about being their manager entitles you to early notice of a still-forming, reversible personal decision; a job search commonly stalls, changes direction, or quietly ends without ever reaching an accepted offer (a general pattern, not a verified statistic for this case) — so pressing for disclosure exposes them to a real, one-sided risk for something that may not happen, while waiting costs you comparatively little.

## What to do

First, privately assess how solid the signal actually is and how you came by it — a public "open to work" flag is a very different thing from a half-overheard call or something a peer passed along, and if the source is one you weren't really supposed to have (monitored email, a glimpsed screen, a confided tip), that changes what you can legitimately act on at all (unverified — check your own company policy and local employment law before using it). Two more things are worth ruling out before you act on anything else: whether they might be interviewing for an internal move rather than leaving the company entirely (this changes everything — you may be able to help directly, and quietly blocking an internal move is one of the more reliable ways to make a team's culture worse), and whether there's anything protected behind this — an unresolved complaint, a denied accommodation request, a harassment or discrimination issue. If there is, this stops being a management judgment call and becomes an HR or legal matter immediately, and none of the rest of this applies until that's handled (unverified — check with HR/legal; not something to assess alone).

Before making any staffing, workload, or opportunity decision differently because of what you know, apply one test: would I make this same call if I didn't know? If not, don't make it — that's the line between reasonable contingency planning and quiet punishment for something unconfirmed.

At your next regular 1:1 (or this week if you're not due for one soon), have a forward-looking conversation — what they'd add or drop about their role, what they've asked you for that you haven't delivered, what would make the next year here worth staying for — and extend the same conversation to your other reports on a similar cadence so it doesn't read as singular. Skim your own notes beforehand for anything they've already asked for and didn't get; that's often most of the answer. In that conversation, or as a standing team norm, make it genuinely low-cost for anyone to tell you they're exploring options — say plainly it won't cost them good work, visibility, or a reference — but only if you can actually back that up; if your organization treats a disclosed search as someone already gone, don't promise something you can't deliver.

Treat documentation, cross-training, and named backups for key work as standing practice for the whole team, tied to a reason that would be true regardless of this one person — a real deadline, a headcount plan, an audit. If you can't state a reason that applies to everyone, you're singling them out and it will show. In the other direction, don't act on the suspicion either: don't pull them off good work, skip them in a comp or promotion cycle they've earned, or quietly exclude them from planning — doing any of that is likely to become the actual reason they leave, and that kind of damage is hard to undo once it starts.

Finally, prepare for the moment they do tell you: thank them, don't perform surprise or hurt, and ask what they need from you and on what timeline. Most managers rehearse the confrontation that never happens and fumble the disclosure that does.

## Risks of this plan

If the signal is wrong — burnout, a health issue, an internal move, or just idle browsing — you'll have spent real effort managing something that isn't happening; the cost stays low only if your response stays generic rather than targeted. Continuity work, even framed as standing practice, can still read as a tell if it's new and suspiciously timed — there's no version of this that's risk-free, only lower-risk. Waiting for them to raise it on their own timeline means you may still get less warning than you'd like if they leave abruptly; that's the real price of not forcing the conversation onto your schedule. And if you tell them disclosure is safe and your own organization doesn't actually treat it that way, you'll have made a promise you can't keep, which costs more trust than never raising the subject at all.

Set against that: this plan is largely reversible. A generic check-in and standard documentation practices cost little and can be adjusted or escalated later as you learn more. The harder-to-reverse mistakes sit on the other side — a direct question that gets denied is a conversation you can't un-have, and a targeted freeze-out that gets discovered is a suspicion they can't un-feel. Even then, the move is a forward-looking question, not a confrontation.
