## Recommendation

Don't choose between going after the incumbent's unhappy customers and finding a niche it ignores. Pick the entry segment instead by a two-part test: the incumbent's cheapest realistic response to losing that segment must cost it more than the segment is worth, and the customers in it must have no entrenched habit or contract locking them to the incumbent. Where a segment clears both, commit to it concentrated rather than spread thin.

## Why

Ten times the resources mostly matters where money converts directly into the fight — price cuts, ad spend, sales headcount, feature parity. It doesn't automatically transfer to a segment the incumbent isn't actually spending much of that budget defending: if a target segment is a small share of the incumbent's business, what it can justify spending to defend that one segment is a small slice of its total war chest, while a concentrated challenger can commit nearly everything it has to that same fight. The 10x gap is real in aggregate and largely beside the point at the segment level, provided the segment is chosen so the incumbent's response stays cheap for the challenger and costly for the incumbent.

The same logic applies to the strong brand. Brand strength isn't uniform across a market — it matters most where buyers can't easily judge quality themselves and bear real personal or professional risk for a bad choice ("nobody gets blamed for picking the market leader"), and matters far less where quality is cheap to verify directly (a trial, a demo, a reversible commitment). It can even work against the incumbent in segments where its reputation reads as expensive, complex, or not built for that buyer. Brand moats are also often bound to a specific channel or sales motion; a brand built through one distribution model doesn't automatically defend the incumbent in a different one.

Neither offered option survives as a standalone plan. Dissatisfaction and switching are not the same thing. The pattern across competitive markets generally (unverified for this specific incumbent) is that most customers who report real dissatisfaction don't act on it by switching to a named competitor — a live prospect is often just as likely to use a competing quote as leverage for a renewal discount, typically the cheaper outcome for the incumbent, as to actually leave. Unhappy customers are, almost by definition, people held in place by the same switching costs or risk-aversion that made the incumbent dominant in the first place. An "ignored" niche, meanwhile, is only a real opportunity if the neglect is structural — built into the incumbent's pricing model, channel commitments, or what its sales organization is paid to chase — rather than simply a market nobody, including the challenger, can profitably serve. A niche ignored only because it's small is a countdown timer, not a moat: prove it's worth something and the incumbent's brand and budget can follow.

That's why the honest answer to "or something else" is a segment defined by what it costs the incumbent to defend, filtered further to customers not already locked in by habit or contract. This isn't a compromise between the two offered options; it reframes both — "unhappy" becomes a source of evidence rather than a target list, and "ignored" becomes a hypothesis to verify rather than an assumption.

This plan rests on one assumption worth naming plainly: that the incumbent will weigh a response against the segment's actual worth the way a rational, margin-conscious business usually does, rather than treating the challenger as an existential threat worth fighting past normal cost logic. Most incumbents, most of the time, behave this way — but not always, and nothing in the situation as described says which applies here. An incumbent that reads a segment as an early signal of a threat to its core business, rather than a containable skirmish, can respond well past what rational short-term cost-accounting would justify: pricing below cost, pressuring shared partners to exclude the challenger, or simply acquiring it early.

Before spending on acquisition, spend one to two weeks on deskwork (public pricing and packaging history, reviews and complaint forums, job postings, partner-program terms, any public numbers, and the challenger's own win/loss and support notes) answering four questions: what actually makes the incumbent dominant here — a network effect, a switching cost, distribution lock-in, scale economics, or mostly brand-as-default-choice, since each implies a different viable segment; what the incumbent's cheapest plausible response to each candidate segment would be (a targeted discount, a bundled feature, pressure on a shared partner, or an acquisition offer) and what that response would cost it outside the segment; who around the incumbent gains or loses if a given segment leaves — the buyer's own champion (who carries personal risk for switching), the incumbent's reps (paid on retention or new bookings), and any shared channel partners (who may lose services revenue or might profit from an alternative) — since that map often predicts whether a response is easy or costly to mount; and how the incumbent has actually behaved the last few times a smaller competitor gained traction — fast, slow, aggressive, or absent. Without a read on that last point, treat the "rational incumbent" assumption above as unverified and plan the downside accordingly.

Follow with three to four weeks of direct conversation: 20-30 interviews split between people currently stuck with the incumbent despite friction, people with no incumbent habit to break at all (new to the category, or actively paying in time or money to work around a gap), and, where possible, channel partners who might profit from working with the challenger instead. Ask about the last real purchase or workaround decision, not hypothetical interest, and ask for a form of payment or commitment, not just a favorable opinion.
